## The question

Nuclear-armed states are integrating machine learning into early warning, intelligence
analysis, targeting, and the communications systems that would carry a launch order. Should
there be a binding rule that a human being, not an algorithm, makes the decision to use a
nuclear weapon — and if so, what would such a rule actually constrain?

## Why it matters

The stability of nuclear deterrence has always rested on decisions made by people under time
pressure with imperfect information. Automating any part of that chain changes the failure
modes: faster, less transparent, and prone to errors that no one in the chain can see. The
episodes in which human judgment stopped a false alarm from becoming a launch — 1983 is the one
everyone cites — are the argument for keeping people in the loop. The argument on the other
side is that people are slow and fallible too, and that an adversary who automates first gains
an advantage.

## Where things stand

On 1 December 2025 the UN General Assembly adopted its first resolution on the subject
(A/RES/80/23), which "demands that, pending the total elimination of nuclear weapons, human
control and oversight is maintained over command, control and communications systems of
nuclear weapons." It passed 118 to 9 with 44 abstentions. The nine against included the United
States, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, Israel and North Korea; China, India and Pakistan
abstained. **No nuclear-armed state voted for it.** The resolution is not binding.

Separately, the United States and China stated in November 2024 that decisions on nuclear use
should remain under human control — a bilateral political statement, not an agreement.

## The case for a binding human-control rule

- The consequences of a nuclear use decision are unique, and delegating any part of it to a
  system whose reasoning cannot be inspected is an unacceptable risk.
- A norm stated now, before the systems are fielded, shapes their design; one stated afterward
  has to be retrofitted.
- The 2025 vote shows the norm has broad support among the states that would suffer the
  consequences without holding the weapons.

## The case against — or for caution

- "Human in the loop" is undefined. Every nuclear state already says a human decides; the
  question is what the human is looking at, how much time the system leaves them, and whether
  they can meaningfully disagree with an automated recommendation. A rule that does not address
  those is a slogan.
- Some uses of AI make nuclear command safer — better early-warning discrimination, faster
  detection of a cyber intrusion — and a blanket rule could forbid them.
- The nuclear-armed states' objection is partly procedural: they want such norms negotiated
  among themselves, not imposed by a General Assembly majority. Whether that is a reason or an
  excuse is itself contested.
