## The question

Should a country with spent nuclear fuel reprocess it — separating uranium and plutonium for
new fuel and immobilizing the rest — rather than storing and eventually burying it intact?
The question has been argued in the United States since the 1970s and is live again in 2026
because of the federal recycling-campus program ([situation
page](/situation/us-spent-fuel-and-the-back-end/)).

## Why it matters

Reprocessing produces separated plutonium, the material a nuclear-security system is built to
guard and account for ([Module 2, sections 5–6](/modules/the-fuel-cycle-and-where-security-lives/)).
It also costs a great deal, and the countries that do it have decades of experience to draw on.
The security community's interest is in making sure the decision is made with the plutonium and
the accountancy problem in view, whichever way it goes.

## The case for recycling

- Spent fuel still contains about 95 percent of its uranium and 1 percent plutonium — energy
  that direct disposal throws away; recycling recovers it.
- Recycling reduces the volume of high-level waste and removes the long-lived actinides from
  it, which proponents argue shrinks the repository problem and shortens the time the waste is
  hazardous.
- A recycling industry is a domestic fuel supply, an industrial base, and — in the 2026 U.S.
  framing — regional jobs and investment.
- Modern processes that keep plutonium mixed with uranium or other actinides are more
  proliferation-resistant than classic PUREX, and safeguards-by-design can make a modern plant
  verifiable.

## The case against

- No fuel cycle removes the need for a repository; the U.S. National Academies said so in 2023,
  and the recycling states are all building one. Recycling changes the waste's form, not the
  requirement.
- Repository size is governed by heat, not volume; separating plutonium and uranium changes
  the heat load only slightly.
- At uranium prices anywhere near recent levels, reprocessing costs substantially more than
  direct disposal by every independent estimate, and plants have run late and over budget
  everywhere they have been built.
- A large reprocessing plant cannot be accounted for to within several significant quantities
  of plutonium a year by material balance alone; "proliferation-resistant" processes offer only
  a modest improvement against a state that owns the plant, in the 2009 judgment of six U.S.
  national laboratories.

## What both sides now agree on

That a geological repository is needed regardless. The leading recycling advocates say so in
their own publications; the disagreement is about scale and sequencing — whether recycling
should shrink the disposal problem before a repository is built — not about whether one is
needed.
