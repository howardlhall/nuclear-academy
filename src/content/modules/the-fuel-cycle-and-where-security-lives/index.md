*This module is built from the author's preparation for a public panel on the fuel cycle held at
the University of Tennessee in August 2026. Everything that changes — inventories, prices,
licensing dockets, the status of particular projects — lives on the
[situation page for the U.S. back end](/situation/us-spent-fuel-and-the-back-end/). This module
keeps what does not change: what the stages are, where the material of concern is, and what the
last fifty years have taught. The site takes no position on whether any country should recycle
its fuel; it does try to state accurately what recycling does and does not do.*

## 1. The cycle in one paragraph

Uranium is dug out of the ground, chemically cleaned up, converted to a gas, enriched so that a
larger share of it is the isotope that fissions, converted back to a solid, and made into fuel.
The fuel runs in a reactor for a few years, during which some of the uranium fissions, some
becomes plutonium, and the fuel fills up with intensely radioactive fission products. Then it
comes out. From there it goes one of two ways: it is stored and eventually buried as it is (the
**once-through** or **open** cycle), or it is chemically taken apart so that the unused uranium
and the plutonium can be made into new fuel, with the fission products separated out for burial
(the **closed** cycle, or **recycling**). Either way, something ends up in a deep geological
repository.

Everything that follows is that paragraph in detail, with attention to one question: at which
stages is there material that could be turned into a weapon, and what does that imply for how the
stage is guarded and measured?

## 2. The front end

**Mining and milling.** Uranium ore is mined, or leached from the ground in place, and processed
into a concentrate called yellowcake (mostly U₃O₈). Natural uranium is 0.7 percent uranium-235;
the rest is uranium-238, which does not sustain a chain reaction with slow neutrons
([Foundations 4](/foundations/04-nuclear-reactions/)). Yellowcake is only mildly radioactive and
is no use for a weapon without the steps that follow. Security here is about tracking bulk
quantities and knowing where they go. The front end is global: a handful of countries mine most of
the world's uranium, and the largest of them, Kazakhstan, is not the one whose reactors consume it.

**Conversion.** Yellowcake is turned into uranium hexafluoride (UF₆), a compound that is a solid
at room temperature and a gas when gently heated. It is the only form in which uranium can be
enriched by the methods in use.

**Enrichment.** The share of uranium-235 is raised — to 3 to 5 percent for the reactors in service
today, to just under 20 percent (**HALEU**, high-assay low-enriched uranium) for many of the new
designs, and to 90 percent or more for a weapon. The commercial method is the gas centrifuge:
thousands of spinning tubes in series, each separating the slightly lighter UF₆ molecules
containing U-235 from the slightly heavier ones containing U-238. The work is measured in
separative work units (SWU), and the important physical fact is this: **most of the work of going
from natural uranium to weapons-grade is done by the time you reach reactor grade.** Going from
0.7 to 4 percent takes about two-thirds of the SWU that going all the way to 90 percent does. That
is why an enrichment plant is inherently a dual-use facility, why the same centrifuge cascade
can in principle be reconfigured, and why enrichment is one of the two stages the safeguards
system watches most closely.

**Fuel fabrication.** Enriched UF₆ is converted to uranium dioxide powder, pressed into ceramic
pellets, sealed into metal tubes, and bundled into assemblies. The product is countable: an
assembly has a serial number, and a reactor operator knows exactly how many it has. Countability
matters for what comes next.

## 3. The reactor

Inside the reactor, three things happen to the fuel at once. Uranium-235 fissions, releasing the
energy the plant sells. Uranium-238 captures neutrons and becomes plutonium-239, some of which
fissions in turn — by the end of a fuel's life a third or more of the energy is coming from
plutonium the reactor made itself. And fission products pile up: strontium-90, cesium-137,
iodine-131, and hundreds of others, most of them intensely radioactive.

The result is **spent fuel**: still about 95 percent uranium by mass, about 1 percent plutonium,
and the rest fission products and heavier elements. Freshly discharged, an assembly is
dangerously radioactive — lethal within minutes at close range without shielding — and it stays
hot, thermally and radiologically, for decades. That radioactivity is a hazard, but it is also,
from a security point of view, a barrier: nobody steals a fresh spent-fuel assembly. The
plutonium in it is unreachable without a heavily shielded chemical plant. This is what the field
calls the **spent-fuel standard**, and it is the reason plutonium *in* spent fuel is treated so
differently from plutonium *separated from* it.

Safeguards at a reactor are comparatively simple, because the material is in countable items.
Inspectors count assemblies, verify serial numbers, seal the pool or cask, and watch the
loading and unloading with cameras. If an item is missing, it is missing. Reactor designs that
handle fuel as a liquid or as hundreds of thousands of pebbles lose that countability, which is
why they need new methods ([Module 1, section 7](/modules/nuclear-security-why-it-matters/)).

## 4. The back end, once-through

In the once-through cycle, spent fuel is cooled in a water pool for years, then moved into
sealed steel-and-concrete dry casks on a pad, and eventually — in principle — moved to a deep
geological repository, several hundred meters underground in stable rock, and left there.

The word "principle" is doing work. As of 2026 no country has yet placed commercial spent fuel in
an operating deep repository, though Finland is at the threshold and Sweden and France have
sites and designs. In the United States the repository program has been stalled since 2010, for
reasons that were legal and political rather than technical, and the fuel sits where it was
made: on the order of 95,000 metric tons at roughly 75 sites, growing by about 2,000 tons a year,
now mostly in dry casks. The figures, the money, and the litigation are on the situation page.

From a security standpoint the once-through cycle has one large virtue: the plutonium never
leaves the spent fuel. Dry casks are a physical-protection problem — a large, heavy, well-marked
target that cannot be moved quickly — but they are not a diversion problem in the safeguards
sense. The material of concern stays behind its own radiation barrier for as long as the fuel
remains intact, which is centuries.

## 5. The back end, closed: reprocessing

In the closed cycle, spent fuel is dissolved in acid and chemically separated into three streams:
uranium (to be re-enriched or stored), plutonium (to be made into mixed-oxide fuel, MOX, or
stored), and everything else (to be immobilized in glass and buried). The process in use at
industrial scale everywhere it is done is **PUREX**, developed in the United States in the 1950s
for weapons production and adapted for civil fuel. Variants that keep the plutonium mixed with
uranium or other elements have been demonstrated at laboratory and pilot scale; none has run at
commercial scale.

Three facts about reprocessing are durable enough to belong here rather than on the dated page.

**It does not eliminate the repository.** Every state that reprocesses is also building or siting
a deep repository. The fission products still have to go somewhere; losses and residues from the
process itself are long-lived waste; and the recycled fuel, when it is spent in turn, is
generally not reprocessed again. The U.S. National Academies put it in one sentence in 2023:
"Regardless of the fuel cycle, a geologic repository will be required." Recycling changes the
*form* and the *volume* of the waste. Whether that is worth doing is the question countries
answer differently; that they still need a repository is not in dispute, and the leading
advocates of recycling say so themselves.

**Volume is the wrong number to argue about.** Reprocessing shrinks the volume of high-level
waste a great deal. But the size of a repository is set by heat, not volume: the waste has to be
spaced so the rock does not overheat, and most of the heat for the first century comes from
cesium-137 and strontium-90, which reprocessing does not remove. Separating out the plutonium and
uranium reduces the heat load only slightly. So "recycling cuts the waste by ninety percent" and
"recycling barely changes the repository" can both be true, because they are about different
things.

**It produces separated plutonium.** This is the security fact. Plutonium in spent fuel is behind
the radiation barrier; plutonium coming out of a reprocessing plant is a powder or a solution
that can be handled by a person in a glove box. Eight kilograms of it is what the IAEA defines as
a **significant quantity** — the amount from which a first weapon could be made. A commercial
reprocessing plant separates several tons of plutonium a year. The world's stock of separated
civil plutonium is now measured in the hundreds of tons and has grown for decades, because
separation has run ahead of the fabrication of MOX fuel to use it. The United Kingdom, which
holds the largest civil stockpile, decided in 2025 to immobilize most of it rather than recycle it
at all.

## 6. Why a bulk plant cannot be exactly accounted for

Here is the point on which everything else in this module turns, and it is a matter of
arithmetic rather than policy.

At a reactor, material is in items, and you count them. In an enrichment or reprocessing plant,
material is in **bulk** — gas in pipes, solution in tanks, powder in hoppers — and you cannot
count it. You *measure* it: weigh the inputs, sample and analyze the outputs, and compare. The
comparison is called the **material balance**, and the number that comes out of it is the
**material unaccounted for**, or MUF: inputs minus outputs minus what is still inside. In a
perfect world MUF is zero. In the real world every measurement has an uncertainty, and the
uncertainties add up.

Do the sum for a plant that separates 8,000 kilograms of plutonium a year. Suppose every
measurement is good to 1 percent — which is very good for chemical analysis of a hot, dirty
solution. The uncertainty on the year's balance is then on the order of 80 kilograms. That is
**ten significant quantities.** The plant could be missing ten weapons' worth of plutonium and the
material balance could not tell you. This is not a criticism of any particular plant or
instrument. It is a property of measuring large flows: the uncertainty scales with the
throughput, and the significant quantity does not.

Safeguards at bulk plants therefore do not rely on the material balance alone. They add
**containment and surveillance** — seals, cameras, and process monitors that watch for material
leaving by any path other than the measured ones — and **near-real-time accountancy**, which
closes the balance every few days instead of once a year so that a discrepancy shows up before
it can grow. Those measures are real and they work. What has never been solved, in the thirty-odd
years since the question was first posed, is how to combine them with the material balance into
a single number that says how confident you are. The honest statement is that a large
reprocessing plant is safeguarded by a layered system whose overall effectiveness is judged, not
computed.

Two consequences follow. First, **safeguards have to be designed into a bulk plant from the
flowsheet up**, not added at licensing: where the measurement points are, how the tanks are
instrumented, where the cameras can see. The field's term is *safeguards by design*. Second, the
question "who is the accountancy regulator for this plant?" has to have an answer before the
plant is built. In the United States, as of 2026, for a commercial reprocessing plant it did not —
see the situation page.

## 7. What other countries have done

The durable lessons, country by country; the numbers are on the situation page.

- **France** reprocesses its uranium-oxide fuel at La Hague and makes MOX, and has done so for
  decades. It is also building a deep repository (Cigéo), and most of what will go into it by
  volume is long-lived intermediate waste, not vitrified high-level waste. Spent MOX fuel is not
  reprocessed again and accumulates.
- **The United Kingdom** reprocessed for fifty years, stopped, and now holds the world's largest
  civil plutonium stockpile, which it has decided to immobilize as waste. It is siting a
  repository.
- **Japan** built a reprocessing plant at Rokkasho that has been under construction or
  commissioning since the 1990s, with cost overruns and delays that have become the standard
  cautionary example. Japan is also siting a repository.
- **Russia** and **China** both reprocess, both at military-origin sites, both with repository
  programs at earlier stages.
- **Finland** and **Sweden** do not reprocess and are the closest to operating deep repositories,
  Finland first.
- **The United States** reprocessed commercially for a few years around 1970, stopped for a
  mixture of economic and non-proliferation reasons, and has since run the once-through cycle
  without completing its back end. In 2026 a federal program is trying to restart recycling on
  Department of Energy sites; whether it succeeds is a current question, not a settled one.

The pattern: reprocessing is a choice some countries have made for reasons of energy security,
industrial policy, or waste form, at a cost that has consistently exceeded the once-through
alternative at prevailing uranium prices; it has not, anywhere, removed the need for a repository;
and it has, everywhere, created stocks of separated plutonium that have to be guarded, accounted
for, and eventually disposed of.

## 8. Four numbers to ask for

When you read a claim about the fuel cycle, these are the questions that separate the durable
from the promotional.

1. **"Reduces waste by X percent" — volume, or heat?** If volume, ask what happens to the
   cesium and strontium. If heat, ask over what period.
2. **"Recycling supplies fuel for advanced reactors" — at what enrichment?** Uranium recovered
   from ordinary spent fuel is barely above natural, about 0.8 to 0.9 percent U-235, and carries
   contaminants that make it harder to enrich. It is not HALEU and does not become HALEU without
   going back through an enrichment plant.
3. **"Demonstrated technology" — at what scale, on what fuel?** Kilograms in a hot cell and
   thousands of tons a year in a plant are different engineering problems. Ask for the largest
   quantity of real commercial spent fuel the process has ever handled.
4. **"Proliferation-resistant" — compared with what, against whom?** Processes that keep
   plutonium mixed with uranium raise the bar for a thief; they do little against a state that
   owns the plant. Six U.S. national laboratories said so in 2009, and nothing since has changed
   the conclusion.

**Explore.** (a) Trace one kilogram of natural uranium through the once-through cycle and say at
which stage it is countable and at which it is bulk. (b) Why does the spent-fuel standard make
dry-cask storage a physical-protection problem rather than a safeguards problem? (c) Redo the
material-balance arithmetic in section 6 for a plant handling 20,000 kilograms of plutonium a
year at 0.5 percent uncertainty. How many significant quantities is the uncertainty? (d) Two
countries both say they have "solved" their waste problem, one by recycling and one by opening a
repository. What would you check in each case?
