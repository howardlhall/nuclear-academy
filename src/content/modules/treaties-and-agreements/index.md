*The nuclear order is not one treaty but a layered set of instruments built over seventy years,
each fixing a problem the last one left. This module is the map. Status that changes — who has
ratified, what has expired, what is being negotiated — is on the
[situation page](/situation/nuclear-forces-and-arms-control/). Dates are given as opened for
signature / entered into force where both matter.*

## 1. Four families

It helps to sort by what a treaty is trying to stop:

1. **Nonproliferation** — stopping more states from getting weapons. The NPT and the
   safeguards system, the nuclear-weapon-free zones, the export-control regimes.
2. **Arms control and disarmament** — limiting or reducing the arsenals that exist. The
   U.S.–Soviet and U.S.–Russian bilateral treaties; the Prohibition Treaty.
3. **Testing** — stopping nuclear explosions. The Partial and Comprehensive Test Ban Treaties.
4. **Nuclear security** — keeping material out of non-state hands. The physical-protection
   convention, the nuclear-terrorism convention, Security Council resolution 1540.

A fifth category is not treaties at all but matters as much: **bilateral cooperation
agreements** and **political commitments**, from a 123 agreement to a summit communiqué.

For each instrument, three questions: *who is bound* (parties), *what is verified* (and by
whom), and *is it in force*. Most public confusion about treaties is confusion about one of
the three.

## 2. Nonproliferation

**The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT).** Opened for signature 1 July
1968; in force 5 March 1970; extended indefinitely in 1995; 191 parties, more than any other
arms treaty. Three bargains, usually called pillars: non-nuclear-weapon states undertake never to
acquire nuclear weapons (Article II) and to accept IAEA safeguards on all their nuclear material
(Article III); all parties undertake to pursue negotiations on disarmament (Article VI); and all
parties have the right to peaceful nuclear technology and undertake to facilitate its exchange
(Article IV). The treaty defines a nuclear-weapon state as one that had tested before 1 January
1967 — the United States, Soviet Union/Russia, United Kingdom, France and China — and binds those
five to the disarmament and cooperation articles but not to safeguards. Four states with weapons
are outside it: India, Pakistan and Israel never joined; North Korea joined in 1985 and announced
its withdrawal in 2003 under Article X, the only state ever to do so. Review conferences every
five years assess it; the 2015, 2022 and 2026 conferences all ended without an agreed document
([Module 1, section 5](/modules/nuclear-security-why-it-matters/)).

**Safeguards agreements.** The NPT's verification is done through separate treaties between
each state and the IAEA: comprehensive safeguards agreements on the INFCIRC/153 model, and
Additional Protocols on the INFCIRC/540 model ([Module 3](/modules/how-safeguards-work/)).
States outside the NPT that receive nuclear supply accept item-specific safeguards on the older
INFCIRC/66 model, covering particular facilities rather than the whole program — the
arrangement India, Pakistan and Israel operate under.

**Nuclear-weapon-free zones.** Regional treaties by which the states of a region renounce
nuclear weapons and the nuclear-weapon states, in protocols, undertake not to use or threaten
to use them against the zone: Latin America and the Caribbean (Tlatelolco, 1967), the South
Pacific (Rarotonga, 1985), Southeast Asia (Bangkok, 1995), Africa (Pelindaba, 1996) and Central
Asia (Semipalatinsk, 2006), plus Mongolia's single-state status. Together they cover the
southern hemisphere and more than a hundred states. Antarctica, outer space and the seabed are
demilitarized by separate treaties (1959, 1967, 1971).

**Export-control regimes.** Not treaties: groups of supplier states that agree, politically, on
common rules for what they will sell and to whom. The **Zangger Committee** (1971) interprets
the NPT's Article III trigger list. The **Nuclear Suppliers Group** (founded 1974 after India's
test; guidelines published 1978, tightened after the Iraq revelations in 1992, and in 2011 for
enrichment and reprocessing transfers) sets the rules most nuclear trade follows, and its
guidelines are published by the IAEA as INFCIRC/254. The **Missile Technology Control Regime**
(1987) does the same for delivery systems. Because they are political arrangements among
suppliers, they bind no one legally and can be changed by consensus — the 2008 exemption that
allowed nuclear trade with India despite its non-membership of the NPT is the standing example
of both their flexibility and its cost.

**Bilateral cooperation agreements.** A state that supplies nuclear technology usually requires
a bilateral agreement setting conditions — safeguards, no explosive use, consent rights over
enrichment, reprocessing and retransfer. In the United States these are **section 123
agreements** under the Atomic Energy Act, and there are about two dozen in force. They are
where a supplier can demand more than the NPT does, which is the subject of the
[open question on enrichment for newcomers](/questions/enrichment-for-newcomer-states/).

## 3. Arms control and disarmament

The bilateral lineage, in order, because each treaty built on the last:

- **SALT I** (1972): the first limits on U.S. and Soviet strategic launchers, and the **ABM
  Treaty** limiting missile defenses. The United States withdrew from the ABM Treaty in 2002.
- **SALT II** (1979): signed, never ratified by the United States, observed informally into the
  1980s.
- **INF Treaty** (1987): eliminated all U.S. and Soviet ground-launched missiles of 500–5,500 km
  range — the first treaty to abolish a whole class, with on-site inspection. The United States
  withdrew in 2019, citing Russian violation.
- **START I** (1991; in force 1994): the first real reductions in deployed strategic warheads,
  with an intrusive verification regime. Expired 2009.
- **SORT** (2002): a three-page treaty limiting deployed strategic warheads, with no
  verification of its own. Superseded.
- **New START** (2010; in force 2011): 1,550 deployed strategic warheads and 700 deployed
  launchers each, with data exchanges and inspections. Extended once, to 2026. Inspections
  stopped in 2020 and data exchanges in 2023; the treaty **expired on 5 February 2026** with no
  successor. As of that date no treaty limits the size of any state's nuclear arsenal
  ([situation page](/situation/nuclear-forces-and-arms-control/)).

Alongside the treaties, three cooperative programs of the 1990s and 2000s did as much for
security as any treaty: the **Cooperative Threat Reduction** ("Nunn–Lugar") program that helped
secure and dismantle former Soviet weapons and material; the **HEU Purchase Agreement**
("Megatons to Megawatts," 1993–2013) under which 500 tons of Russian weapons HEU were blended
down and burned in U.S. reactors; and the **Plutonium Management and Disposition Agreement**
(2000), under which each side was to dispose of 34 tons of weapons plutonium — suspended by
Russia in 2016.

**The Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW).** Adopted 2017; in force 22 January
2021; more than 70 parties. It prohibits its parties from developing, possessing, hosting,
using or threatening to use nuclear weapons, and provides a path for a weapon state to join and
disarm. No nuclear-armed state and no state under a nuclear umbrella has joined; its
supporters regard it as the disarmament pillar the NPT never delivered, its critics as a
treaty among states that had nothing to give up. Its first review conference is in 2026.

## 4. Testing

**The Partial Test Ban Treaty** (1963) banned nuclear explosions in the atmosphere, in space and
under water — the first arms-control treaty of the nuclear age, driven by fallout. Testing went
underground.

**The Threshold Test Ban Treaty** (1974) and the **Peaceful Nuclear Explosions Treaty** (1976)
capped underground tests at 150 kilotons; verification protocols were not agreed until 1990.

**The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty** (1996) bans all nuclear explosions. It has 187
signatories and 178 ratifications but has **never entered into force**, because its Annex 2
requires ratification by 44 named states with nuclear technology, and nine have not: the
United States, China, Egypt, Iran and Israel have signed but not ratified; India, Pakistan and
North Korea have not signed; Russia ratified in 2000 and revoked its ratification in 2023. The
treaty's organization nonetheless operates an **International Monitoring System** of seismic,
hydroacoustic, infrasound and radionuclide stations that detected every North Korean test. A
moratorium has held among the recognized weapon states since 1996 and among all states since
2017; whether it holds is an [open question](/questions/should-the-testing-moratorium-hold/).

## 5. Nuclear security

These instruments address the threat this site is mostly about: material in the wrong hands.

**The Convention on the Physical Protection of Nuclear Material** (1980; in force 1987) originally
covered only nuclear material in international transport. Its **2005 Amendment** (in force 8 May
2016) extended it to material in domestic use, storage and transport and to the protection of
facilities against sabotage, making it the only legally binding international instrument on
physical protection of civil material. About 130 states are party to the amended convention.

**The International Convention for the Suppression of Acts of Nuclear Terrorism** (2005; in force
2007) obliges parties to criminalize the possession and use of radioactive material with intent
to cause harm, and to cooperate in prosecution and extradition.

**UN Security Council Resolution 1540** (2004), adopted under Chapter VII and therefore binding
on every UN member, requires all states to refrain from supporting non-state actors seeking
nuclear, chemical or biological weapons, to adopt domestic laws to that end, and to control
related materials. Its committee's mandate runs to 2032. It is the instrument that reaches
states outside every treaty.

**Soft law.** The IAEA's Code of Conduct on the Safety and Security of Radioactive Sources
(2003) and its Nuclear Security Series guidance are not binding but are the standards
regulators use, and the four **Nuclear Security Summits** (2010–2016) turned much of that
guidance into national commitments and produced the removal of HEU from more than a dozen
countries.

## 6. The ones that failed, and what they teach

- **The Agreed Framework** (1994) froze North Korea's plutonium program in exchange for fuel
  oil and light-water reactors; it collapsed in 2002 over a covert enrichment program, and North
  Korea tested in 2006.
- **The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action** (2015) capped Iran's enrichment at 3.67 percent,
  limited its stockpile and centrifuges, and gave the IAEA its most intrusive access anywhere,
  in exchange for sanctions relief. The United States withdrew in 2018; Iran stepped away from
  its limits from 2019, enriched to 60 percent from 2021, and reduced access thereafter. Its
  formal termination date was October 2025. The 2025–26 strikes and the September 2026 Board
  referral are on the situation page.
- **The INF Treaty and the ABM Treaty**, above.

The pattern is not that treaties fail; most of the instruments on this page are in force and
observed. It is that an agreement whose verification is withdrawn, or whose political basis
one side renounces, loses its content while its text survives — which is why this module asks
of every instrument not only what it says but whether it is verified and whether it is alive.

**Explore.** (a) For each of the four families, name the instrument that does the most work
today and the one that has failed. (b) Explain why the NSG's 2008 India exemption could be
granted without amending any treaty, and what that says about export controls. (c) The CTBT is
signed by 187 states and observed by all of them, yet is not in force. What difference does
entry into force make? (d) Resolution 1540 binds states that have signed nothing. Why did the
Security Council take that route in 2004, and what are its limits?
