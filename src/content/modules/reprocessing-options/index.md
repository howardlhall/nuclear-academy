*This module follows [Module 2](/modules/the-fuel-cycle-and-where-security-lives/), which
explains why reprocessing is a security question, and takes no position on whether any
country should do it. It describes processes at the level of the open technical literature and
the regulators' documents: what each does, not how to run it.*

## 1. What reprocessing is for

Spent fuel comes out of a reactor as a mixture: about 95 percent uranium, about 1 percent
plutonium, and a few percent of fission products and minor actinides, all locked in a ceramic
inside metal cladding and intensely radioactive. Reprocessing takes that mixture apart
chemically so that the uranium and plutonium can be reused and the rest can be packaged for
disposal. Every option below is a different way of doing the taking-apart, and the options
differ mainly in **what comes out in the plutonium stream and how clean it is**. That is why a
security site cares which one a country chooses.

## 2. PUREX: the process that exists

**PUREX** (plutonium–uranium reduction extraction) was developed in the United States in the
early 1950s for weapons plutonium production and has been the basis of every commercial
reprocessing plant since. In outline:

1. **Chop and dissolve.** Fuel assemblies are sheared into pieces and the ceramic fuel is
   dissolved in hot nitric acid, leaving the cladding hulls as waste.
2. **Extract.** The acid solution is contacted with an organic solvent (tributyl phosphate in a
   kerosene-like diluent) that takes up uranium and plutonium and leaves the fission products
   behind. Repeated contacting in columns or mixer-settlers gives high purity.
3. **Partition.** Plutonium is chemically shifted to a form the solvent will not hold, and
   stripped out separately from the uranium. Each is purified further and converted to an oxide.

The fission-product raffinate is concentrated and, in modern plants, **vitrified** — melted
into borosilicate glass in steel canisters — for eventual deep disposal. Cladding hulls,
solvents and process wastes are intermediate-level waste, and in volume they are most of what a
reprocessing plant sends to a repository.

PUREX works: it is reliable, its chemistry is understood in detail, and it has been run at
scales up to more than a thousand tons of fuel a year at La Hague in France. Its defining
feature for this site is that step 3 produces **pure separated plutonium** — a powder that can
be handled in a glove box, from which a significant quantity is eight kilograms
([Module 3](/modules/how-safeguards-work/)). Every other process on this page was invented
because of that feature.

## 3. The alternatives, and how far each has got

The OECD Nuclear Energy Agency's expert group rated the readiness of the options on the
standard nine-level scale, and the ratings are the most useful single fact about them.

**Modified aqueous processes — COEX, UREX and relatives (TRL about 6).** Variants of PUREX in
which the plutonium is never fully separated: it is kept with uranium (COEX, the process the
French developed for their MOX line), or with uranium and neptunium, or with other actinides
(the UREX family developed in the U.S. national laboratories). The chemistry is close to PUREX
and the equipment is similar. What has been demonstrated is pilot-scale operation on real
spent fuel; in the United States, the largest integrated campaign on actual commercial fuel
totals about 4.5 kilograms, at Oak Ridge. No plant using any of them has been built.

**Pyroprocessing (TRL below 6).** Not aqueous at all: the fuel is converted to metal and
dissolved in a molten salt at several hundred degrees, and uranium and a mixture of plutonium
with other actinides are recovered on electrodes. Developed for the metallic fuel of fast
reactors, it has one operating example: the treatment of the fast-reactor fuel from the EBR-II
program at Idaho, which has processed about 5.5 of some 25 tons over 27 years. The national
laboratory's own 2023 report to the NRC says the process "has not been developed past the
engineering scale." South Korea has a long-running research program.

**Advanced partitioning.** Processes to pull minor actinides (americium, curium) out of the waste
for burning in fast reactors, reducing the long-lived heat and radiotoxicity of what is
buried. Laboratory to pilot scale, in France, Japan and elsewhere; a research field rather than
an option a utility can buy.

The pattern: the processes that avoid separated plutonium are precisely the ones that have not
been run at scale, and the one that has been run at scale is the one that produces it.

## 4. "Proliferation-resistant" reprocessing

The modified processes are described as proliferation-resistant because the plutonium never
appears alone: a thief would get a uranium–plutonium mixture, or a mixture with highly
radioactive actinides, and would need further chemistry to make it usable. That is a real
benefit against theft.

It is a much smaller benefit against the state that owns the plant. In 2009 six U.S. national
laboratories assessed the question and concluded that the alternatives offer "only a modest
improvement in reducing proliferation risk over existing PUREX technologies, and these modest
improvements apply primarily for non-state actors." A state can add the extra chemistry; the
plant's owner already has the expertise, the facility and the material. The claim to keep in
view, then, is that process choice can raise the bar for a thief, and that against a
determined state the bar is set by safeguards, not by chemistry.

## 5. The safeguards problem is the same for all of them

Whatever the process, a reprocessing plant is a **bulk-handling facility**: tons of plutonium
a year in solution or salt, measured rather than counted. The material-balance uncertainty
scales with throughput and comes to many significant quantities a year at commercial scale
([Module 2, section 6](/modules/the-fuel-cycle-and-where-security-lives/)). The mitigations —
solution monitoring on every tank, near-real-time accountancy that closes the books every few
days, containment and surveillance on every path — are the same for PUREX and for its
variants; the pyroprocess is harder, because molten salt is not easily sampled and its
composition is not easily measured.

Two implications hold for every option. **Safeguards by design:** the measurement points, tank
instrumentation and camera lines of sight have to be in the flowsheet, not added later. And
**a defined regulator:** somebody has to own the material accountancy rules for the plant before
it is built. In the United States in 2026, for a commercial plant, that was not settled — the
NRC's material-accounting rule for Category I material excludes reprocessing plants by name,
and the 2026 proposed rules on reprocessing licensing and physical protection did not change
that ([situation page](/situation/us-spent-fuel-and-the-back-end/)).

## 6. The U.S. history in six dates

- **1966–1972.** A commercial PUREX plant at West Valley, New York, reprocesses about 640 tons
  of fuel, then closes for upgrades that never happen; the site becomes a cleanup project that
  is still running.
- **1977.** President Carter defers commercial reprocessing indefinitely, citing proliferation
  concerns after India's 1974 test; a nearly complete plant at Barnwell, South Carolina, never
  operates.
- **1981.** President Reagan lifts the ban. No company builds a plant; the economics do not
  support one, and the once-through cycle becomes the de facto U.S. policy.
- **2001–2009.** The Global Nuclear Energy Partnership proposes recycling with "proliferation-
  resistant" processes; a dedicated NRC rulemaking begins, is narrowed in 2013, suspended in
  2016 and discontinued in 2021.
- **2025.** Executive orders on nuclear energy direct the use of "all available legal
  authorities," and DOE begins soliciting recycling proposals on its own sites, offering DOE
  authorization rather than NRC licensing.
- **2026.** The NRC proposes a one-step licensing pathway for reprocessing plants under
  10 CFR Part 70 and a companion physical-protection rule; DOE signs non-binding memoranda with
  five states for "Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campuses." Status on the situation page.

## 7. Elsewhere

France runs the world's largest civil reprocessing operation at La Hague and recycles the
plutonium as MOX in its own reactors, once. The United Kingdom reprocessed at Sellafield for
fifty years, closed the Magnox plant in 2022 and THORP in 2018, and now holds the largest civil
plutonium stockpile, which it has decided to immobilize. Japan's Rokkasho plant, begun in the
1990s, has been postponed repeatedly and is not yet in full operation. Russia reprocesses at
Mayak, a weapons-program site. China has a pilot plant and is building larger ones. India
reprocesses for its fast-reactor program. Every one of those states is also siting or building a
deep repository, because reprocessing does not remove the need for one.
