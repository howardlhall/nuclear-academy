*This module describes enrichment at the level of the public record — the histories, the
regulators' backgrounders, the IAEA's published safeguards approaches. It contains no design or
operating parameters, and none should be read into it. It follows
[Module 2](/modules/the-fuel-cycle-and-where-security-lives/) and
[Foundations 4](/foundations/04-nuclear-reactions/).*

## 1. The problem stated

Natural uranium is 0.7 percent uranium-235, the isotope that fissions with slow neutrons, and
99.3 percent uranium-238, which mostly does not. Most reactors need the U-235 share raised to
3–5 percent; the newer designs want up to 20 percent; a weapon wants 90 percent or more.
Raising that share is **enrichment**, and it is hard for one reason: the two isotopes are the
same element. They form the same compounds, react the same way, and differ only in mass — by
three neutrons in 238, a little over one percent. Every enrichment technology is a way of
exploiting that one percent, and every one of them has to do it over and over, because a single
pass barely changes anything.

## 2. The technologies

**Electromagnetic separation.** Ionize uranium, accelerate it, bend it in a magnetic field; the
lighter isotope bends more. The calutrons at Oak Ridge did this for the Hiroshima bomb, one
atom at a time, at colossal cost in electricity and labor. Abandoned after 1945 everywhere
except, it turned out, in Iraq, whose secret calutron program came to light in 1991 and helped
prompt the Additional Protocol ([Module 3](/modules/how-safeguards-work/)).

**Gaseous diffusion.** Push uranium hexafluoride gas through a porous barrier; the lighter
molecules pass slightly more often. Repeat through thousands of stages in a plant the size of a
small town, consuming as much electricity as a city. This was the workhorse of the Cold War —
Oak Ridge, Paducah and Portsmouth in the United States, Pierrelatte in France, plants in the
Soviet Union, Britain and China. All are now closed; the last, Paducah, shut in 2013.

**Gas centrifuge.** Spin uranium hexafluoride in a rapidly rotating cylinder; the heavier
molecules drift toward the wall, the lighter toward the axis, and a temperature-driven flow
along the axis concentrates the difference. Connect many centrifuges in a cascade and the
small per-machine effect compounds. The centrifuge uses a small fraction of the electricity of
diffusion, and a plant can be modular and comparatively compact — the qualities that made it
the commercial technology of choice and, at the same time, the technology of choice for a
covert program. Every commercial enrichment plant operating today is a centrifuge plant.

**Laser separation.** Tune a laser to a wavelength that one isotope absorbs and the other does
not, and pull the excited atoms or molecules out. Several variants have been developed over
fifty years; one has been licensed for commercial deployment in the United States. Laser
methods are attractive because a plant could be smaller and use less energy per unit of
product, and worrying for exactly the same reason; the safeguards literature treats them as a
technology to watch rather than one that has changed the picture yet.

A few other methods — thermal diffusion, aerodynamic nozzles, chemical exchange — were tried
and abandoned or used only briefly.

## 3. The number that makes enrichment dual-use

Enrichment effort is measured in **separative work units (SWU)**, a quantity that depends on how
much material is fed in, how enriched the product is, and how much U-235 is left in the
depleted "tails." The one fact from the SWU arithmetic that everyone in this field carries:

> **Going from natural uranium to reactor-grade uses most of the separative work needed to go
> all the way to weapons-grade.**

Roughly two-thirds of the work to reach 90 percent is spent in reaching 4 percent; the rest of
the way is comparatively short because the feed is already concentrated and the quantity of
material shrinking. Starting from 20 percent — HALEU — the remaining distance is shorter still.
This is why an enrichment plant built for fuel is inherently a latent weapons capability; why
the same cascade can be reconfigured, by re-piping, to run its product back through as feed;
why the world treats enrichment above 20 percent as a bright line; and why the newer reactor
designs' appetite for HALEU is a nonproliferation question and not only a supply one.

## 4. Who enriches, and how it spread

Commercial-scale enrichment is operated by a small number of states and consortia: the United
States (one operating commercial plant, owned by a European consortium), France, the
Netherlands, Germany and the United Kingdom (through the Urenco group), Russia (the largest
capacity in the world), China, and, at smaller scale, Japan, Brazil, Argentina, India,
Pakistan, and Iran. North Korea enriches covertly. Several other states have had programs and
stopped.

The technology spread along two paths. The declared path: state programs in the nuclear-weapon
states and a European consortium that developed the centrifuge for civil fuel. And the
undeclared path: a metallurgist who had worked at a Urenco subcontractor took centrifuge
designs to Pakistan in the 1970s, built Pakistan's program on them, and then ran a commercial
network that sold designs, components and know-how to Libya, Iran and North Korea
([Module 1, section 6](/modules/nuclear-security-why-it-matters/)). The centrifuge is the
technology whose proliferation the export-control system was rebuilt to stop, and the
Nuclear Suppliers Group's 2011 rules on enrichment and reprocessing transfers are the result.

Two facts about the supply side bear on current policy. Russia has for decades supplied a large
share of the world's enrichment services, including nearly all commercial HALEU; the United
States and Europe are now spending heavily to build capacity of their own, and the U.S. ban on
Russian enriched-uranium imports (with waivers) is part of that. And enrichment services are a
world market with spare capacity, so there is no economic case for a newcomer state to build
its own plant — which is why a newcomer that insists on one is asked what else it wants it for
([Open question: enrichment for newcomer states](/questions/enrichment-for-newcomer-states/)).

## 5. Safeguarding an enrichment plant

An enrichment plant is a bulk-handling facility ([Module 2, section
6](/modules/the-fuel-cycle-and-where-security-lives/)), so material accountancy alone cannot
close the balance to within a significant quantity, and the inspector's questions are
specific:

- **Is the plant producing above its declared enrichment level?** Answered by measuring the
  product — with instruments on the pipes, by sampling cylinders, and above all by
  **environmental swipe sampling**: uranium particles on surfaces carry the enrichment of the
  gas that deposited them, and a cascade that has ever run at high enrichment leaves particles
  that say so. This technique has caught undeclared high enrichment more than once.
- **Is all the feed accounted for and all the product where it should be?** Cylinder
  accountancy: weighing, tagging and tracking every UF₆ cylinder in and out, with surveillance
  on the loading areas.
- **Are there more centrifuges than declared?** Design-information verification: inspectors
  compare the plant as built and as running with what the state declared, and look for
  cascade halls, feed stations or piping that are not on the drawings.
- **Is there an undeclared plant somewhere else?** This is the question a comprehensive
  safeguards agreement alone cannot answer and the Additional Protocol was written for. A
  centrifuge plant is compact, quiet, and has no radiation signature to speak of; its
  detectability rests on the Additional Protocol's access rights, on export-control tracing of
  the components it needs, and on intelligence.

The limits are real. The agreed approaches work well at a cooperative plant; they work poorly
at one whose operator restricts access, removes cameras, or moves centrifuges; and they do not
work at all at a plant nobody has declared. The record of the last twenty-five years is a
record of all three cases.
