*Adapted from a lecture by the author and Jackson Stanley for a fusion safety and regulation
course organized by the Clean Air Task Force and the University of Tennessee in July 2026. The
regulatory status of fusion changes month to month; the current state is on the
[situation page](/situation/nuclear-forces-and-arms-control/#regulatory-status-items-referred-from-module-1).
This module keeps the structure of the problem.*

## 1. Two dates

Fusion power has been "fifty years away" for seventy years. That joke hides a fact worth
starting with: humanity first released fusion energy on 1 November 1952, at Enewetak Atoll, in a
test called Ivy Mike — the first full-scale thermonuclear explosion, on the order of ten
megatons. Our first fusion "reactor" was a weapon. Whatever else is said about fusion's
proliferation risk, it cannot be said that the two subjects have nothing to do with each other,
and it is the reason safeguards for fusion cannot be an afterthought.

The second date is now. Private fusion companies have raised billions of dollars; several are
building machines they describe as pilot plants; the United States has rewritten its regulatory
framework for them; and the International Atomic Energy Agency has begun scoping what
safeguards for fusion might look like without yet adopting anything. Commercial fusion is
arriving faster than its safeguards. This module is about the gap.

## 2. The system we have was built for fission

The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty and the safeguards system that verifies it were designed
around one idea: **fissile material**. Enriched uranium and plutonium are what the treaty
controls, what a comprehensive safeguards agreement lets the IAEA account for, and what every
inspection technique — item counting, material balance, seals, cameras, environmental sampling
— is built to track ([Module 2, section 6](/modules/the-fuel-cycle-and-where-security-lives/)).
Decades of experience with reactors, enrichment plants and reprocessing plants stand behind it.

A fusion machine, in normal operation, runs on deuterium and tritium — two isotopes of hydrogen
— and produces helium and neutrons. It uses no fissile material and makes none. So as the rules
are written today, a pure deuterium–tritium fusion plant is **not subject to IAEA safeguards at
all**: there is no "nuclear material," in the treaty's sense, to safeguard. That is not an
oversight anyone has decided to fix; it is simply what the definitions say.

Two features of the system matter for what follows. **Safeguards bind states, not facilities.**
The obligation flows from a state's treaty commitment; the state declares its facilities and
accepts verification on its territory; a developer does not make its own deal with the Agency.
So any workable approach has to let each state show its compliance credibly, and industry has
to help shape the rules in every country that will build fusion, not just one. And **the
baseline agreement sees only what is declared.** Detecting undeclared activity — which is
fusion's real concern, as the next section explains — is the job of the Additional Protocol,
which is voluntary and not in force everywhere ([Module 1, section 4](/modules/nuclear-security-why-it-matters/)).

## 3. Why the misuse potential is real anyway

If fusion makes no fissile material, why is this a nonproliferation subject at all? Three
reasons.

**Neutrons breed.** A deuterium–tritium plant is an intense neutron source — that is what makes
it work. Neutrons that land on uranium-238 make plutonium-239; neutrons that land on thorium-232
make uranium-233 ([Foundations 4](/foundations/04-nuclear-reactions/)). A fusion machine
deliberately fitted with a blanket of fertile material would be a production reactor of a new
kind, and analyses of realistic tokamak designs have found that the quantities are not trivial.
Nothing about normal operation does this; the concern is a purpose-built or covertly modified
facility — an *undeclared* activity, exactly the case the baseline safeguards agreement is
weakest at finding.

**Tritium is a weapons material.** It is used to boost the yield of fission weapons, it is
scarce, it decays with a 12-year half-life so that stockpiles have to be continuously renewed,
and a commercial fusion industry would handle it by the kilogram. Deuterium is a heavy-water
moderator and a boosting material too, and lithium-6 is the feedstock from which tritium is bred.
Each moves through a commercial supply chain, and a state could skim material from that chain.
None of the three is "nuclear material" under the treaty, and accountancy for them is nowhere
near as developed as it is for uranium and plutonium.

**Components are dual-use.** Much of what goes into a fusion machine — tritium-handling systems,
lithium isotope separation, high-power lasers or magnets, neutron diagnostics — has uses in a
weapons program. Export controls already cover some of it; the rest is a question that trade at
scale will force.

Put together: the proliferation risk from fusion in normal operation is low, lower than fission —
**but not zero, and lower than fission only if safeguards are designed in.** That is the finding
of the most-cited academic treatment of the question, Goldston and Glaser's 2012 paper, and the
authors of this module concur. The design-in window is now, while the first commercial machines
are still on paper.

## 4. Where the tritium comes from

Fusion is often described as freeing the energy system from the fission fuel cycle. Its fuel
supply says otherwise, at least for now.

Tritium is rare in nature and decays quickly, so a fusion plant has to be supplied with it.
Today's supply comes from fission: mainly from CANDU heavy-water reactors, which make tritium
as a by-product in their heavy water, and in the United States from tritium-producing rods
irradiated in a commercial power reactor for the weapons program. The world's civil inventory
is a few tens of kilograms. A fleet of fusion plants would exhaust it quickly.

The long-term answer is for each plant to **breed its own tritium** from lithium-6 in a blanket
around the machine. That in turn calls for lithium enriched in the lighter isotope — a separation
step with its own dual-use character. Either way, the fusion fuel cycle reaches back into
fission reactors and into isotope-enrichment technology. Anyone drawing the boundary of "what
needs safeguarding" around a fusion industry has to draw it wider than the fusion machine.

## 5. What fusion actually produces

Set misuse aside and ask what a fusion plant leaves behind. Two things.

**Activation products.** The neutrons that carry the energy out of the plasma make the
surrounding structure radioactive. Activated steels and other components become
intermediate-level waste — bulky, but with no transuranic elements and no fission products,
which is the difference from fission that matters most for disposal. With sensible material
choices, most activated steel can be cleared or recycled after about a century.

**Tritium.** Radioactive hydrogen contaminates everything it touches, permeates metals, and is
environmentally mobile as tritiated water. It drives much of the waste-handling burden of a
fusion plant. Its saving grace is its half-life: after roughly thirty years most tritium-bearing
waste has decayed below low-level-waste limits, so interim storage is a real strategy in a way it
is not for fission waste.

The result is a waste stream that is manageable rather than a show-stopper: no high-level waste
at disposal after cooldown, no repository of the kind the fission back end requires. That is the
tractable half of the problem, and it should not be oversold or undersold.

## 6. How regulators are choosing to treat it

The United States redrew the map in 2024. The ADVANCE Act defined material produced by fusion as
**byproduct material**, and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission is therefore regulating fusion
machines under its materials framework — 10 CFR Part 30 — rather than the power-reactor rules
written for fission. The proposed rule appeared in February 2026; guidance scales oversight with
the size of the tritium and activation inventories; and security requirements are to be decided
case by case rather than imposed as a class. It is a lighter, risk-informed path than fission
licensing, and it settles one question: **fusion will not be licensed like a fission reactor**,
at least in the United States. Whether and when the rule becomes final is tracked on the
situation page.

The United Kingdom and the European Union (through ITER and Euratom) have gone facility by
facility, with detailed activation and tritium waste protocols and a distinctive Euratom legal
framework. Other fusion nations are taking their own paths. There is convergence on the waste
question and none yet on verification.

## 7. Where things stand

**Converging:** fusion will not be licensed like fission; activation and tritium waste are
manageable.

**Unresolved:** there is no international safeguards approach designed for fusion; how tritium
export controls apply as trade scales; and who writes the verification playbook, and when.
The IAEA has begun scoping work and published successive editions of a fusion outlook; nothing
has been adopted.

The argument this module makes is that **industry has to lead** — bring developers, regulators
and the IAEA to the same table, shape workable protocols in every fusion nation rather than
waiting to react, and turn practical experience into verifiable standards — while the IAEA
designs a fusion-tailored approach and states universalize the Additional Protocol. The
alternative is to repeat the fission experience of the 1960s: build first, verify later, and
spend the next half-century catching up.
