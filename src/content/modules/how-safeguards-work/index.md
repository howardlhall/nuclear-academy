*This module is the author's own synthesis of the public safeguards literature — the IAEA's
statute, glossary and model agreements, and the standard textbooks — written to sit between
[Module 1](/modules/nuclear-security-why-it-matters/), which says why verification matters, and
[Module 2](/modules/the-fuel-cycle-and-where-security-lives/), which shows where in the fuel cycle
it bites. Current figures — how many states have which agreements, where verification has been
lost — are on the [situation page](/situation/nuclear-forces-and-arms-control/).*

## 1. The one-sentence version

**Safeguards are the set of measures by which the International Atomic Energy Agency verifies
that a state is keeping its promise not to use nuclear material for weapons.**

Every word carries weight. *Verifies*: the system does not trust and does not accuse; it
checks. *State*: the obligation is the state's, not a company's or a plant's. *Nuclear
material*: uranium, plutonium and thorium in defined forms — not tritium, not reactor designs,
not scientists. *Promise*: safeguards exist because of a treaty commitment; they are the
verification arm of the Non-Proliferation Treaty, not a police force with independent
jurisdiction.

And one thing they are not: safeguards are not physical protection. They do not guard material
against thieves; that is the state's job under a different body of law ([Module 1, section
2](/modules/nuclear-security-why-it-matters/)). Safeguards watch the *owner*.

## 2. The legal chain

Three links.

**The treaty.** A non-nuclear-weapon state that joins the NPT undertakes never to acquire
nuclear weapons (Article II) and to accept IAEA safeguards on all its nuclear material
(Article III). The five states the treaty recognizes as nuclear-weapon states have no such
obligation, though each has volunteered some facilities for inspection.

**The agreement.** Each non-nuclear-weapon state concludes a **comprehensive safeguards
agreement** with the IAEA — a legally binding treaty between the state and the Agency, on a
model text known as INFCIRC/153. It obliges the state to keep accounts of all its nuclear
material, to report them, and to admit inspectors to check them. States with little or no
material can hold a simplified version (a "small quantities protocol").

**The inspection.** Under the agreement the IAEA sends inspectors, installs equipment, takes
samples, and reports its conclusions to its Board of Governors each year. The Board, made up of
member states, is where a finding of non-compliance is made and, if warranted, referred to the
UN Security Council. The Agency verifies; the states judge.

The chain matters because it fixes what safeguards can reach. The Agency can go where the
agreement says; it can measure what the state has declared and what the agreement lets it look
for; and it can report. It cannot compel, and it cannot enforce.

## 3. What is being verified: the significant quantity and the timeliness goal

The system is built around two numbers.

The **significant quantity (SQ)** is the IAEA's estimate of the approximate amount of material
from which the possibility of making a nuclear explosive device cannot be excluded: **8 kilograms
of plutonium, 25 kilograms of uranium-235 contained in highly enriched uranium,** 75 kilograms
of U-235 in low-enriched or natural uranium (the amount that would have to be enriched further),
and figures for uranium-233 and thorium. The SQ is the unit in which safeguards think: the
question is never "is any material missing?" but "could an SQ have been diverted without
detection?"

The **timeliness detection goal** is how quickly a diversion should be caught, and it depends on
how close the material is to weapons use: on the order of **a month** for separated plutonium
or HEU, **three months** for plutonium still locked in spent fuel, and **a year** for natural or
low-enriched uranium, including fresh reactor fuel. Together the two numbers set the inspection effort: material
that is one chemical step from a weapon gets visited every few weeks; material that is years
away gets an annual check.

Neither number is a physical constant. Both are Agency conventions, publicly stated in the IAEA
Safeguards Glossary, and both are argued about — the plutonium SQ in particular is thought by
many analysts to be conservative in the wrong direction, that is, larger than what a competent
program would need. The system's designers chose them to be defensible and stable rather than
precise.

## 4. What inspectors actually do

Three families of technique, used together.

**Nuclear material accountancy** is the foundation. The state keeps books — a ledger of every
batch of material at every facility — and reports them. Inspectors verify the books against
reality: they count items (fuel assemblies have serial numbers), weigh containers, measure
enrichment with hand-held detectors, and take samples for laboratory analysis. At the end of a
period they close a **material balance** for each material balance area: what came in, what went
out, what is still there. The difference is the **material unaccounted for**, and the question is
whether it is consistent with measurement uncertainty or not ([Module 2, section
6](/modules/the-fuel-cycle-and-where-security-lives/) does the arithmetic for a bulk plant).

**Containment and surveillance** watch the paths material could take between measurements.
Seals on containers, cask lids and doors show whether anything was opened; cameras record fuel
movements around the clock; radiation monitors at portals and on process lines flag material
going where it should not. These do not measure anything; they establish that nothing happened
that the books do not record. In a bulk plant they carry much of the load.

**Environmental sampling and analysis** look for what the books cannot show. Swipe samples from
surfaces inside and around facilities pick up particles that reveal, for instance, what
enrichment level a cascade has actually run at — the technique that exposed undeclared work in
several states. Satellite imagery, open-source information and trade data round it out.

The output is not a yes or no. It is an annual **safeguards conclusion** for each state, drawn
by the Agency and reported to its Board, in one of a small number of standard forms.

## 5. What the system can conclude — and the Additional Protocol

Here is the distinction that a reader has to carry away, because it decides what every
safeguards headline means.

Under a comprehensive safeguards agreement alone, the Agency verifies the material the state
has **declared**. It can therefore conclude, at most, that *declared nuclear material remained in
peaceful activities*. It has limited means to look for material or facilities the state never
mentioned — and that, since Iraq's undeclared program came to light in 1991, is the case that
matters.

The **Additional Protocol** (model text INFCIRC/540, 1997) is the answer. A state that adopts it
gives the Agency much wider information — about the whole fuel cycle, including mining,
research and manufacturing — and much wider access, including short-notice access to any
location on a site and to undeclared locations. With those tools the Agency can draw the
**broader conclusion**: that *all nuclear material remained in peaceful activities* — declared
and undeclared alike. Getting the broader conclusion takes years of evaluation after the
protocol enters into force; having the protocol is necessary but not sufficient. The Additional
Protocol is voluntary: many states have adopted it, some prominent ones have not, and a
cooperation agreement can be written without requiring it — a live policy question in 2026 (see
the situation page).

So when a report says the Agency "could not draw a conclusion" for a state, that is a finding
of *absence of assurance*, not a finding of diversion. When it says a state is in
**non-compliance**, that is a Board determination that the state has broken its agreement —
by failing to declare material or activities, or by refusing access — which is different again
from a finding that a weapon is being built. The three statements are routinely conflated in
public discussion and should not be.

## 6. Where safeguards have been lost, and why

Verification depends on **continuity of knowledge**: an unbroken chain of measurements,
seals and records from one inspection to the next. Break the chain and the Agency cannot say
what happened in the gap, however good its instruments. The chain can break in three ways.

- **The state withdraws.** North Korea expelled inspectors and left the treaty in 2003; nothing
  has been verified there since, and its arsenal is estimated, not counted.
- **The state restricts access.** Iran's step-by-step reduction of access after 2019, and its
  removal of cameras in 2022, left the Agency unable to re-establish a baseline for centrifuge
  production — a gap the Agency has said it cannot close.
- **The facilities are attacked.** When safeguarded facilities in Iran were bombed in 2025 and
  2026, the Agency lost the ability to verify the material declared there, including a large
  stock of highly enriched uranium. Military action does not divert material, but it destroys
  the measurement, and the safeguards system has no procedure for verifying a bombed site the
  state does not open.

In each case the system did what it is built to do: it reported, truthfully, that it no longer
knew. That is the system working, not failing. Whether the world then acts on the report is
outside the Agency's hands.

## 7. What it costs and who pays

The Agency's safeguards budget is on the order of 150 to 200 million euros a year, spread
over roughly 180 states, more than a thousand facilities, and a few hundred inspectors — less,
as the standard comparison goes, than the budget of a large city's police department. It is
funded by member-state assessments, with a heavy reliance on a handful of large contributors,
and it has been under recurrent financial strain. The scale is worth knowing when you read that
safeguards "failed" somewhere: the system is asked to give the world confidence about every
declared gram of nuclear material on Earth for the price of a mid-sized hospital.
