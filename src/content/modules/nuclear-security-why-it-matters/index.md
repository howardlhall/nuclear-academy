*This module is adapted from a lecture to first-year nuclear engineering students at the
University of Tennessee. It keeps the lecture's argument and most of its examples, drops the
material that dates in months (which lives in the site's current-situation pages), and adds the
sources a reader would want to check. Each section ends with three questions to test yourself.*

## 1. Why nuclear security, and why now

The world is building nuclear reactors again. After three decades in which the United States
opened almost no new plants and much of the industrial base that had built the earlier ones
quietly dispersed, electricity demand is rising, governments have relearned what carbon-free
baseload power is worth, and dozens of reactor designs — large and small, familiar and novel —
are moving from drawing board to licensing. At the same time the people who built and ran the
first generation are retiring faster than they are being replaced.

Every reactor, every kilogram of fuel, and every design idea in that expansion has a security
dimension. That is the first thing to understand about the subject: nuclear security is not a
specialty bolted onto the side of the nuclear enterprise by people in uniforms. It is built into
the choice of fuel, the layout of a plant, the way material is weighed and tracked, the way data
is handled, and the way people are vetted and trained. An engineer who does not think about it is
making security decisions anyway — badly.

The field organizes itself around four questions, and they are worth memorizing because every
job in it answers one of them:

1. How do we **assure** that nuclear material and technology are where they should be, doing what
   they should do, and properly protected?
2. How do we **detect** activity outside the bounds of appropriate use?
3. How do we **respond** effectively if something bad happens?
4. How do we **assess** honestly what we know — and what we only think we know?

The first is the work of material control, accounting, and physical protection. The second is
the work of safeguards inspectors, detector designers, and analysts. The third is the work of
emergency response and nuclear forensics. The fourth is the hardest and the least glamorous, and
it is the reason the other three ever improve.

**Check yourself.** (a) Which of the four questions does an international safeguards inspector
mainly answer? (b) Why is a reactor designer "making security decisions anyway"? (c) What has
changed since about 2020 that makes the workforce question urgent?

## 2. Security, safety, safeguards, surety — and why the words get confused

Four words, four different jobs. The distinctions matter because the institutions, the law, and
the engineering are different for each, and because in most of the world's languages two or more
of them collapse into one word.

**Security** is the prevention and detection of, and response to, theft, sabotage, unauthorized
access, or illegal transfer of nuclear material or technology. The threat is a person who intends
harm [1].

**Safety** is prevention of, and protection from, unplanned or unanticipated accidents and
incidents involving nuclear material and facilities. The threat is a failure — of equipment, of
procedure, of judgment — with nobody intending anything [2].

**Safeguards** is verification: the system of accounting, inspection, and analysis by which the
International Atomic Energy Agency and national authorities confirm that nuclear material is being
used peacefully and that a state is complying with its agreements. The concern is a state's
intent, and the tool is measurement [3].

**Surety** is a term used by nuclear-weapon states, the United States among them, for the
combined safety, security, and reliability of nuclear weapons and the systems that control them
[4]. It sits outside the civil enterprise but shares its vocabulary and much of its science.

Now the complication. English happens to have two words, *safety* and *security*, for two of
these ideas. Of the six official languages of the United Nations — Chinese, English, French,
Russian, and Spanish under General Assembly resolution 2(I) of 1946, with Arabic added by
resolution 3190 (XXVIII) in 1973 — four use a single word for both: French *sécurité*, Spanish
*seguridad*, Russian *безопасность*, Chinese *安全* [5]. Arabic distinguishes them, with *himaya*
for protection and *aman* for safety. An international treaty, a training course, or a plant
procedure written in one language and read in another can lose the distinction entirely, and the
confusion is not academic: a "safety culture" and a "security culture" ask different things of the
same people, and a program that has one is often assumed, wrongly, to have the other.

A useful test for any measure you encounter: *who is on the other side of it?* If the answer is
"nobody — physics, weather, or fatigue," it is safety. If the answer is "someone who wants this to
go wrong," it is security. If the answer is "a government that has promised not to," it is
safeguards.

**Check yourself.** (a) A reactor's containment building protects against both an accident and
an attack; which of the four words applies to each purpose? (b) Why does it matter that French has
one word for safety and security? (c) Which of the four concerns a state's intentions rather than
a person's or a machine's?

## 3. A short history: secrecy before the bomb, and what the Cold War taught

Security in the nuclear field is as old as fission. In the months after the discovery was
published in early 1939, physicists in several countries recognized from the open literature what
a chain reaction could mean, and some of them began, on their own initiative, to withhold results
and to speak in code. Glenn Seaborg later recalled the plutonium project's early habit of calling
the new element "copper" — which worked until the chemists needed real copper, at which point the
metal became "honest-to-god copper" [6]. The point of the anecdote is not the joke but the
instinct behind it: the people closest to the science understood at once that information about
it was a strategic quantity.

The Manhattan Project and the Cold War that followed built that instinct into a system: remote
sites with controlled access, personnel security investigations, formal classification of
information, compartmentalization so that few people saw the whole picture. Most of it worked.
The part that did not is the more instructive.

The United States program was penetrated repeatedly. Klaus Fuchs, a theoretical physicist at Los
Alamos, passed weapon design information to the Soviet Union. The ring around Julius and Ethel
Rosenberg, with David Greenglass and Harry Gold, passed more. Theodore Hall and Saville Sax were
identified only decades later, through the Venona decrypts of Soviet cable traffic [7]. George
Koval, a Soviet military intelligence officer who worked inside the project's Oak Ridge and Dayton
sites, was not publicly identified until Russia awarded him a posthumous Hero of Russia medal in
2007. The Soviet program was penetrated in its turn — Oleg Penkovsky, Adolf Tolkachev, and Dmitri
Polyakov each supplied the West with material of the first importance, and each was executed
after discovery.

The lesson is not that security failed. Fission physics was going to be rediscovered anyway, and
the bomb was built in several countries by people who never saw a stolen page. The lesson is that
**personnel security and counterintelligence are permanent requirements**, not wartime measures
to be relaxed when the emergency passes. Every institution that handles nuclear material or
sensitive information will be a target for as long as it does so, and its defenses have to assume
that some of the people inside it are not what they appear to be.

**Check yourself.** (a) What does the "copper" story show about scientists' attitude to
information in 1939–42? (b) Name two Soviet sources inside the U.S. program and two Western
sources inside the Soviet one. (c) Why is the espionage record an argument for permanent
personnel security rather than for more secrecy?

## 4. What actually impedes proliferation

Here is the central fact of the subject, and it is counter-intuitive to most people who come to
it fresh: **secrecy about how a nuclear weapon works is not the main thing standing between the
world and more of them.**

The basic principles have been public for a long time. A gun-type weapon fires one sub-critical
piece of highly enriched uranium into another; it is simple and robust enough that the design used
at Hiroshima was never tested beforehand. An implosion weapon compresses a sub-critical core of
plutonium or highly enriched uranium to a super-critical density with precisely shaped
conventional explosives; it is more demanding and is the only route that works for plutonium.
That much has been in encyclopedias for decades. In 1967 the Lawrence Livermore laboratory ran
what it called the Nth Country Experiment: three young physicists with no weapons background were
asked to produce a credible design using only the open literature. They did, in about two and a
half years, and the laboratory's summary report was released under the Freedom of Information Act
[8]. J. Robert Oppenheimer is often quoted as saying that the only real secret was that it works.

Two analysts put the consequence plainly in 2004: the secrets of weapon design "were revealed long
ago," and "the only significant barrier remains access to fissile material: highly enriched
uranium (HEU) or plutonium" [9]. Four years later the *Washington Post* reported that the A. Q.
Khan smuggling network had obtained, and may have distributed, blueprints for an advanced weapon
[10]. Design information leaks. What cannot be downloaded is a critical mass of the right
material.

So what stands in the way? Three things, in descending order of importance:

1. **Access to fissile material** — highly enriched uranium or plutonium, in quantity, in a
   usable form. Producing either requires industrial facilities (enrichment plants or reactors
   plus reprocessing) that are large, expensive, slow, and hard to hide, and that is exactly why
   the world's safeguards system concentrates on them. The atlas on this site shows where they
   are.
2. **Engineering capacity** to turn a design into a working, deliverable device — a much larger
   job than the physics, involving materials, precision manufacture, and testing.
3. **Confidence**, which usually means testing, and testing is detectable.

The implication for how the world defends itself follows directly. Information control has its
place, but the primary tools are **safeguards, detection, material control and accounting, and
intelligence** — measuring, tracking, and protecting material, and noticing when it moves. That is
engineering and science, and it is the kind of work the rest of this site is about.

**Check yourself.** (a) What did the Nth Country Experiment demonstrate, and in what year? (b)
Rank the three barriers to proliferation and say why fissile material is first. (c) Why do
safeguards focus on enrichment and reprocessing rather than on reactors in general?

## 5. The treaty and the map of proliferation

The nuclear age began with one state. By the time the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty entered
into force on 5 March 1970 there were five — the United States (1945), the Soviet Union (1949),
the United Kingdom (1952), France (1960), and China (1964) — and the treaty recognized exactly
those five as nuclear-weapon states while binding every other party never to acquire the weapons
and binding the five to pursue disarmament and to share peaceful technology [11]. The bargain was
preceded by Atoms for Peace (1953), which opened access to nuclear technology, and by the creation
of the International Atomic Energy Agency (1957), which would verify its peaceful use.

The treaty has slowed proliferation without stopping it. India tested in 1974 and called the
device peaceful; Israel is universally understood to have acquired weapons in the same era and has
never acknowledged them; Pakistan tested in 1998; North Korea, having joined and then withdrawn
from the treaty, tested in 2006. Nine states hold nuclear weapons today. Against that, the record
also holds reversals: South Africa built a small arsenal, dismantled it, and joined the treaty in
1991; Belarus, Kazakhstan, and Ukraine gave up the Soviet weapons on their territory in the 1990s.

The Cold War also produced close calls and accidents that belong in any honest account — the
Cuban missile crisis of 1962; the Able Archer exercise of 1983; weapons lost or broken at Tybee
Island in 1958, Goldsboro in 1961, Palomares in 1966, and Damascus, Arkansas, in 1980 — each a
reminder that surety is a live problem for the states that possess these weapons, not a solved
one.

The numbers today, from the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute's count as of
January 2025 [12]: roughly 12,240 warheads worldwide, about 9,600 of them in military stockpiles
and about 3,900 deployed. Russia (5,459) and the United States (5,177) hold about nine in ten.
China has at least 600 and has been adding on the order of a hundred a year since 2023, with some
350 new missile silos under construction — the first major new strategic build-up since the Cold
War. France has about 290, the United Kingdom 225, India 180, Pakistan 170, Israel 90, and North
Korea around 50. For three decades after 1991 the global total fell as the two largest arsenals
were dismantled; that trend has now reversed. The last remaining treaty limiting United States and
Russian strategic forces, New START, expired in February 2026 with nothing to replace it.

**Check yourself.** (a) Which five states does the NPT recognize as nuclear-weapon states, and
why those five? (b) Name four states that acquired weapons after 1970 and two that gave them up.
(c) What changed in the global warhead count after about 2020?

## 6. Proliferation as a business, and the non-state threat

Two developments since the Cold War changed the shape of the problem.

The first was the discovery that proliferation could be run as a business. Abdul Qadeer Khan,
the metallurgist regarded as the father of Pakistan's enrichment program, confessed in 2004 to
having run for years an international network that sold centrifuge designs, components, and
weapon-related information to Libya, Iran, and North Korea [13]. The network bought parts from
suppliers on three continents, shipped through intermediaries, and answered to no government's
policy. It was a new kind of threat — trans-national, commercial, and deniable — and the response
to it is why export controls, supply-chain due diligence, and personnel security now matter to a
nuclear engineer as much as reactor physics does.

The second was the arrival of non-state actors with declared nuclear ambitions. Al Qaeda tried to
buy uranium in Sudan in the early 1990s [14]; Osama bin Laden described acquiring weapons of mass
destruction as a religious duty in 1999; a 2003 fatwa from a Saudi cleric asserted their use was
permissible. In 2014 the Islamic State's seizure of Mosul put radioactive sources at the
university and hospitals there, briefly, outside anyone's control. What such a group would most
plausibly build is not a sophisticated weapon but one of two cruder things: an improvised nuclear
device — most likely gun-type, which requires highly enriched uranium — or a radiological
dispersal device, a conventional explosive packaged with a radioactive source such as cobalt-60,
caesium-137, iridium-192, or strontium-90, whose effect is contamination and fear rather than
blast [15].

The problem is hard for reasons that have nothing to do with physics. A group of that kind has a
small signature, uses unconventional delivery, and cannot be deterred the way a state can. The
only strategy that works against it is denial of material — which brings the argument back to
section 4.

And material has reached the market. The IAEA's Incident and Trafficking Database, to which 145
states report, recorded 4,626 incidents of nuclear and other radioactive material out of
regulatory control between 1993 and 2025 [16]. Most involve small quantities, lost sources, or
contaminated scrap; a minority involve trafficking and intent; a handful involve weapons-usable
material. Highly enriched uranium was seized in Georgia in 2003, 2006, 2010, and 2016; in 2011 a
group in Moldova offered a kilogram of it for sale; other documented cases occurred in Germany,
Bulgaria, and Turkey. The quantities were small. The demonstration of intent and capability was
not.

One more fact places the problem on the map. Kazakhstan alone produced 39 percent of the world's
mined uranium in 2024, and with Uzbekistan the two Central Asian producers supplied about 45
percent [17]. The front end of the fuel cycle is global, and so, necessarily, is its security.

**Check yourself.** (a) What made the A. Q. Khan network a new kind of threat? (b) Why is an
improvised nuclear device more likely to be gun-type than implosion-type? (c) What does the ITDB
record show, and what does it not show?

## 7. The terrain ahead

The lecture this module comes from was titled *why it matters for your career*, and the reason it
matters is that the field is about to change faster than it has in fifty years. Several trends
are converging, and each of them creates work.

**New nuclear.** Reconstituting and expanding fuel-cycle capacity after decades of atrophy; new
countries entering nuclear power for the first time; and a generation of reactor designs whose
operating paradigms differ from the large light-water reactor the safeguards system grew up
around. Molten-salt reactors circulate fuel as a liquid; pebble-bed reactors handle fuel as
hundreds of thousands of TRISO-coated spheres rather than countable assemblies; microreactors are
small enough to move. Each is harder to account for by the methods that work for pellets in metal
tubes, and each needs new instruments and new approaches.

**The HALEU economy.** Many of those designs use fuel enriched to just under 20 percent uranium-235
— high-assay low-enriched uranium — rather than the 3 to 5 percent of today's fleet. HALEU is
harder and more expensive to make, comes in a wider variety of forms, and is a more attractive
feedstock for a state that intended to enrich further. It changes the economics of enrichment
and the calculus of safeguards at the same time.

**Fusion.** Fusion is a serious engineering enterprise again, and it raises questions the
safeguards system has not had to answer: where the tritium for start-up comes from, how
lithium-6 breeding is managed, and whether an intense neutron source could be misused and how
that would be detected.

**Artificial intelligence and autonomy.** AI lowers technical barriers to design and analysis on
both sides; deepfakes and disinformation complicate the attribution of any event; and the
question of AI in nuclear command and control raises new stability risks. Low-cost uncrewed
systems — drones — have attacked nuclear-adjacent infrastructure in the war in Ukraine and must
now be assumed in the design basis of every new facility. Space, commercial and military, is a
new domain for both threats and tools.

**Great-power competition.** Strategic stability was bipolar for most of the nuclear age. It is
not now. China's arsenal is growing faster than any other; several regional powers hold weapons;
the arms-control architecture is eroding; verification tools are under strain. This is the world
in which the next generation of nuclear engineers will work.

What all of that demands can be said briefly. New safeguards and security methods and instruments
have to be developed and proved, preferably with industry rather than at arm's length from it.
Fast, disruptive development has to be married to the deliberate, stable operation that the
"three S's" — safety, security, safeguards — require. And the work cannot live in stovepipes:
research, regulation, and operations have to keep current with one another across a broader set
of challenges than the field has faced before. It is a sprint that turns into a marathon, and it
needs both capability and continuity in the people who run it.

**Check yourself.** (a) Why is a pebble-bed reactor harder to safeguard than a light-water
reactor? (b) What makes HALEU a security question as well as an economic one? (c) Name two ways
AI changes the nuclear security landscape.

## 8. Who does this work

Nuclear security is done by engineers, scientists, and policy analysts in roughly six kinds of
place: the U.S. Department of Energy's National Nuclear Security Administration and its national
laboratories and production sites; the Nuclear Regulatory Commission; the defense agencies and
services; the International Atomic Energy Agency and the wider safeguards community; industry —
utilities, reactor vendors, and fuel-cycle companies; and the policy, intelligence, and analytic
institutions that need technically trained people. The roles carry names like safeguards engineer,
forensic analyst, detection-system designer, fuel-cycle specialist, resilient-design engineer, and
arms-control analyst.

Nuclear forensics is worth a paragraph of its own because it answers the question people ask
first after any incident: *who made this material?* The answer comes from analytical chemistry —
gamma spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, actinide chemistry in hot cells, metallurgical examination
— combined with reactor physics and fuel-cycle knowledge to read a material's history from its
isotopic and chemical signatures. It is a field where the skills of a nuclear engineering degree
map directly onto the work.

The one requirement that runs through every one of these jobs: the security side of the nuclear
enterprise needs people who understand *both* the physics, chemistry, and engineering of reactors,
fuel, and materials, *and* the policy, legal, and human dimensions of security. Neither half is
sufficient alone.

**Check yourself.** (a) Which agency verifies peaceful use internationally, and which regulates
civil nuclear facilities in the United States? (b) What question does nuclear forensics answer,
and with what tools? (c) Why does the field need people fluent in both the technical and the
policy dimensions?
