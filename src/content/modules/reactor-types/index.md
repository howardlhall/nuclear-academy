*A reactor is a machine for sustaining a chain reaction and taking the heat out. Every design
answers the same three questions differently, and the answers decide not only how the plant
performs but how it is safeguarded and guarded. This module assumes
[Foundations 4](/foundations/04-nuclear-reactions/) and sits beside
[Module 2](/modules/the-fuel-cycle-and-where-security-lives/). It stays at the level of what the
IAEA and national regulators publish about reactor classes; it does not describe any particular
plant's protection.*

## 1. Three choices define a reactor

**Fuel.** What fissions. Natural uranium (0.7 percent U-235), low-enriched uranium (3–5
percent), HALEU (up to 20 percent), or in a few research and naval designs, highly enriched
uranium. Plutonium, as mixed-oxide fuel, in some. Thorium, bred to uranium-233, in a handful of
experiments.

**Moderator.** Most reactors need slow neutrons, because uranium-235 fissions far more readily
when hit by a slow neutron than a fast one. The moderator is the material that slows them:
ordinary ("light") water, heavy water, or graphite. A **fast reactor** has no moderator and runs
on fast neutrons, which changes everything about its fuel and its physics.

**Coolant.** What carries the heat away: water (light or heavy), a gas (carbon dioxide or
helium), a liquid metal (sodium, lead), or a molten salt. The coolant sets the operating
temperature and pressure, and with them the engineering of the plant.

Combine the three and you have a reactor family. Two consequences follow for this site's
subject. The fuel choice decides what safeguards have to track and how attractive it is to a
thief. And the moderator and fuel together decide **how much plutonium the reactor makes and
of what quality** — the question that makes some reactor types a nonproliferation concern and
others much less so.

## 2. The families in service

**Light-water reactors (LWRs).** Ordinary water as both moderator and coolant; low-enriched
uranium oxide fuel in metal-clad assemblies. Two variants, the pressurized-water reactor (PWR)
and the boiling-water reactor (BWR), account for about nine in ten operating power reactors
worldwide — the U.S., French, Chinese, Russian (VVER), Korean and Japanese fleets among them.
From a safeguards point of view they are the easy case: the fuel is countable, it stays in
the core for years, the reactor has to be shut down and opened to refuel, and the plutonium it
produces is high-burnup "reactor-grade" — usable in a weapon in principle but far from ideal.
The safeguards system grew up around the LWR, and most of its methods assume one.

**Heavy-water reactors (PHWRs).** Heavy water (deuterium oxide) as moderator, which absorbs so
few neutrons that the reactor can run on **natural, unenriched uranium**. The CANDU design is
the main example, in Canada, India, South Korea, Romania, China, Argentina and Pakistan. Two
features matter here. Because they use natural uranium, they need no enrichment plant — a
plus. But they are refueled continuously, on-line, with fuel bundles pushed through pressure
tubes while the reactor runs, so there is no shutdown to watch and the fuel does not sit in a
countable core; safeguarding them takes bundle counters and continuous surveillance rather
than an annual inventory. And because the fuel spends a short time in the reactor, the
plutonium it makes is closer to weapons quality than an LWR's. India's first nuclear device
used plutonium from a heavy-water research reactor supplied for peaceful purposes.

**Graphite-moderated reactors.** Graphite moderator with gas or water cooling. The British
Magnox and advanced gas-cooled reactors and the Soviet RBMK (the Chernobyl type) are the power
versions; the first production reactors at Hanford and Windscale were graphite piles built to
make plutonium, and the design lineage shows. Most are retired or retiring; a few RBMKs still
operate in Russia. Graphite reactors can run on natural uranium and, like heavy-water reactors,
some refuel on-line.

**Research reactors.** Small, usually water-cooled and -moderated, used for neutron beams,
isotope production, training and testing rather than power. There are more than 800 in the
IAEA's register, in over 70 countries — far more countries than have power reactors. Many were
built with **highly enriched uranium** fuel, and a decades-long international program has
converted most of them to LEU and repatriated the HEU; the remaining HEU-fueled research
reactors are a standing item on the nuclear-security agenda. Research reactors are also where
most countries' nuclear expertise begins.

**Fast reactors.** No moderator; fuel enriched to 15–20 percent or containing plutonium;
cooled by liquid sodium or lead. Fast neutrons are captured by uranium-238 efficiently enough
that a fast reactor can be built to **breed** more plutonium than it consumes, which was the
original point of the design, or to burn plutonium and other long-lived actinides. Russia
operates two commercial fast reactors; China, India and others have programs; the United States
and Europe built and closed several. A fast breeder with a uranium blanket is the reactor type
that makes the highest-quality plutonium most directly, and it is safeguarded accordingly.

**Naval reactors.** Compact PWRs, in several navies fueled with HEU, that power submarines and
carriers. They sit outside civil safeguards entirely, and the arrangement under which a
non-nuclear-weapon state (Australia, under AUKUS) may acquire them without IAEA safeguards
during naval use is an open question in the safeguards system.

## 3. The new generation

The designs now moving through licensing and demonstration differ from the LWR in ways that
matter for this site. The site's [situation page](/situation/nuclear-forces-and-arms-control/)
tracks which have actually started up.

**Small modular reactors (SMRs).** LWRs scaled down to tens or a few hundred megawatts and built
in a factory rather than on site. The physics and the fuel are familiar; what changes is the
number of sites — more plants in more places, some in countries new to nuclear power — and the
staffing model, which security regulators are still working out. Many SMR designs plan to use
HALEU.

**High-temperature gas-cooled reactors.** Helium coolant, graphite moderator, and fuel in the
form of **TRISO** particles — tiny kernels of uranium wrapped in ceramic layers — packed either
into pebbles or into prismatic blocks. Pebble-bed reactors handle hundreds of thousands of
pebbles that circulate through the core continuously; the fuel is not countable in the LWR
sense, and safeguards need new methods ([Module 1, section 7](/modules/nuclear-security-why-it-matters/)).
The fuel's ceramic coating, on the other hand, makes the plutonium in it very hard to extract,
which is a security advantage.

**Molten-salt reactors.** Fuel dissolved in a liquid salt that is also the coolant, circulating
through the core and heat exchangers. There are no fuel assemblies at all; the inventory is a
liquid whose composition changes continuously, and some designs include on-line chemical
processing of the salt — which is, in safeguards terms, a small reprocessing plant attached to
the reactor. Molten-salt designs are the hardest case the safeguards system has yet been asked
to think about, and the thinking is still in progress.

**Microreactors.** Reactors of a few megawatts or less, sometimes transportable, intended for
remote sites, military bases or industry. Their distinctive security question is mobility: a
reactor that can be moved can be stolen, and one deployed far from any guard force has to
protect itself. Several reached first criticality in the United States in 2026 under Department
of Energy authorization rather than NRC licensing, which leaves open which body's security rules
apply.

## 4. What "proliferation-resistant" means and does not mean

Every new design is described by its developers as proliferation-resistant. The phrase has a
meaning, but it is a comparison, not a property.

A reactor is more resistant to the extent that: its fuel is unattractive (low enrichment;
plutonium buried in high-burnup spent fuel or in a ceramic that resists chemistry); its fuel is
countable and rarely handled; it does not need an enrichment or reprocessing plant to run; and
it is hard to modify for producing weapons material without the modification being visible.
It is less resistant to the extent of the opposite: HEU fuel, on-line refueling, short
irradiation, a breeding blanket, or an attached chemical process.

Two cautions. Resistance is against a **particular adversary**: a design that stops a thief may
do nothing against the state that owns the plant, which can change the fuel, the irradiation
time, or the blanket at will. And resistance is **relative to the alternative**: every reactor
makes plutonium, so the question is never whether but how much, of what quality, and how
reachable. Six U.S. national laboratories made the point about advanced fuel cycles in 2009,
and it holds for reactors: the improvements are real and modest, and mainly against non-state
actors. The material barrier remains the material barrier.

## 5. Three questions to ask about any reactor

1. **What is the fuel, and where does it come from?** Enrichment level, form, and supplier. HALEU
   in particular has been a supply-chain and a nonproliferation question at once.
2. **How is it refueled, and can the fuel be counted?** Batch and countable (LWR); continuous
   and countable-with-effort (PHWR, pebble bed); continuous and uncountable (molten salt). This
   decides what safeguards have to look like.
3. **What does it make, and how hard is that to get at?** Plutonium quantity and quality; whether
   it stays behind the spent-fuel radiation barrier; whether any processing is attached.

Answer those three and you know most of what the safeguards inspector, the security planner and
the export-control officer will want to know about the plant — before anyone has asked about
its electricity.
