## 1. What "decay" means

An unstable nucleus does not sit still forever. At some moment — you cannot predict which — it
rearranges itself into a more stable nucleus and throws something out to carry away the extra
energy. That event is **radioactive decay**, and the things thrown out are **radiation**.

Two words to keep straight. The nucleus you start with is the **parent**. The nucleus it turns
into is the **daughter**. The daughter may be stable, or it may be radioactive too, in which case
it decays in its turn — a chain. Unit 2 mentioned uranium-238 taking fourteen hops to reach
lead-206; each hop is one decay, and each nucleus along the way is the daughter of the one before.

Decay changes the nucleus, so it usually changes the element. This is the thing chemistry cannot
do. No furnace, acid, or battery will turn carbon into nitrogen. Carbon-14 does it on its own.

## 2. The bookkeeping rule

Every decay obeys two rules that let you work out the daughter without memorizing anything:

- **Nucleons are conserved.** The total number of protons plus neutrons is the same before and
  after. (A does not change unless a whole nucleon leaves.)
- **Charge is conserved.** The total electric charge is the same before and after.

Write the parent on the left, the daughter and whatever was emitted on the right, and make both
rules balance. That is all a "decay equation" is.

## 3. The four main kinds of decay

### Alpha decay (α)

The nucleus throws out an **alpha particle**: two protons and two neutrons stuck together, which
is the same thing as a helium-4 nucleus. So the daughter has **Z − 2** and **A − 4**.

> uranium-238 → thorium-234 + α
> (Z: 92 → 90; A: 238 → 234)

Alpha decay is how the heaviest nuclei — the ones at the top of the chart — shed weight. On the
chart, an alpha decay is a hop two boxes down and two boxes left. Alpha particles are heavy and
slow for radiation; a sheet of paper or the outer layer of your skin stops them. They are
dangerous mainly if the material gets inside you.

### Beta-minus decay (β⁻)

A neutron inside the nucleus turns into a proton, and the nucleus throws out an **electron**
(here called a beta particle) plus a tiny neutral particle called an antineutrino that you can
ignore for now. Z goes **up by one**; A does not change, because a neutron became a proton and
the nucleon count is the same.

> carbon-14 → nitrogen-14 + β⁻
> (Z: 6 → 7; A: 14 → 14)

This is the decay for nuclides with **too many neutrons** — the ones on the right-hand side of the
valley of stability in Unit 2. On the chart, β⁻ decay is a hop one box up and one box left,
diagonally toward the valley. Beta particles travel farther than alphas; a few millimeters of
aluminum stops them.

### Beta-plus decay and electron capture (β⁺, EC)

The mirror image. A proton turns into a neutron, so Z goes **down by one** and A stays the same.
The nucleus does it in one of two ways: it throws out a **positron** (a positively charged
electron — antimatter, and yes, that is the same positron in a PET scan), or it pulls in one of
the atom's own electrons, which is **electron capture**. Either way the daughter is the same.

> fluorine-18 → oxygen-18 + β⁺
> (Z: 9 → 8; A: 18 → 18)

This is the decay for nuclides with **too few neutrons** — the left-hand side of the valley. On
the chart it is a hop one box down and one box right, again toward the valley.

### Gamma emission (γ)

Sometimes a decay leaves the daughter with extra energy, like a bell still ringing. The nucleus
gets rid of it by emitting a **gamma ray** — light of very high energy, with no mass and no
charge. Nothing about Z or A changes. Gamma rays usually come out right after one of the decays
above, almost as part of it. They are the most penetrating kind of radiation: it takes centimeters
of lead or a meter of concrete to stop most of them, which is why gamma sources are the ones
handled behind shielding.

A few nuclides get stuck in the "ringing" state for a long time — seconds, hours, or years —
before emitting the gamma. Those are the **isomers** the explorer lists; technetium-99m, the
workhorse of hospital imaging, is one.

### The rarer ones

Very heavy nuclei can also split roughly in half on their own (**spontaneous fission** — Unit 4),
and nuclides far from the valley can spit out a bare neutron or proton. The explorer colors these
separately. You will not need them to follow the rest of this site.

## 4. Half-life: the idea

Here is the fact that makes radioactivity strange. You cannot say when a particular carbon-14
nucleus will decay. It might go in the next second or in ten thousand years. There is nothing
inside it counting down, and nothing you can do — heat it, squeeze it, react it with something —
changes the odds.

What you *can* say is the odds. Every carbon-14 nucleus has the same chance of decaying in any
given year, and that chance never changes. So if you have a large number of them, a predictable
fraction decays each year, and the amount left follows a smooth curve.

The **half-life** is the time it takes for half of them to decay. For carbon-14 it is about
5,700 years. Start with a million carbon-14 atoms:

| Time | Half-lives passed | Carbon-14 left |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 1,000,000 |
| 5,700 years | 1 | 500,000 |
| 11,400 years | 2 | 250,000 |
| 17,100 years | 3 | 125,000 |
| 22,800 years | 4 | 62,500 |

Notice that it never reaches zero on paper — each step halves what is left — and notice that half
of a big number is a big number, but half of a small one is small. After ten half-lives about a
thousandth is left; after twenty, about a millionth.

Half-lives run from billionths of a second to billions of years. The explorer gives one for every
box. A short half-life means the material is intensely radioactive but gone quickly; a long
half-life means it barely decays at all and will be around a very long time. Uranium-238's
half-life of 4.5 billion years is about the age of the Earth, which is why roughly half of the
U-238 the Earth started with is still here.

Unit 5 does the arithmetic properly — including what to do when the time is not a whole number of
half-lives.

## 5. Activity: counting decays

Chemists measure a substance by mass or by number of atoms. For a radioactive substance the
useful measure is often **how many decays happen per second**, which is called the **activity**.
The unit is the **becquerel (Bq)**: one becquerel is one decay per second. An older unit you will
still see, the **curie (Ci)**, is 37 billion becquerels — the activity of about one gram of
radium-226.

Activity depends on two things: how many atoms you have, and how fast they decay. A short
half-life and a long half-life can give the same activity if the long-lived one has many more
atoms. Your own body has an activity of a few thousand becquerels, mostly from potassium-40 and
carbon-14, and always has.

## 6. Where you meet it

- **Smoke detectors** contain a speck of americium-241, an alpha emitter, that keeps a tiny
  current flowing across an air gap; smoke interrupts it.
- **Bananas** are slightly radioactive because potassium, which they are rich in, is 0.012 percent
  potassium-40. So are you, for the same reason.
- **Medical scans** use short-lived nuclides made to order: technetium-99m for most imaging,
  fluorine-18 for PET scans. Short half-lives are chosen on purpose, so the dose is gone in hours.
- **Radon** is an alpha-emitting gas made by the decay of uranium in rocks and soil; it seeps into
  basements and is the largest source of natural radiation dose for most people.
- **Carbon dating** works because living things keep taking in fresh carbon-14 until they die, and
  then the clock runs down at a known rate.

## Where this comes from

The decay modes and conservation rules are in any introductory nuclear physics text. Half-lives
quoted here are from the evaluated data behind the explorer (carbon-14 about 5,700 years,
uranium-238 about 4.47 billion years). The becquerel and curie are defined by the International
System of Units and by the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission's glossary respectively; one curie
is exactly 3.7 × 10¹⁰ becquerels by definition. Potassium-40's natural abundance of 0.0117 percent
and the typical whole-body activity of a few thousand becquerels are standard figures in health
physics references. Radon as the largest natural dose source is from the U.S. EPA and the National
Council on Radiation Protection's population-dose reports.
