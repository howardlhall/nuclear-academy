## 1. Everything is made of atoms

Take any object — a pencil, a glass of water, the air you are breathing — and imagine cutting it
into smaller and smaller pieces. Eventually you reach pieces that cannot be cut any further
without turning into something else. Those pieces are **atoms**. There are about 100 different
kinds, and every kind is called an **element**. Gold is an element. So are oxygen, carbon, iron,
and uranium. Water is not an element, because you can take it apart into two elements, hydrogen
and oxygen.

Atoms are small. A single drop of water holds more atoms than there are grains of sand on every
beach on Earth. You will never see one with your eyes, but everything about how matter behaves —
why iron rusts, why helium balloons float, why some rocks are radioactive — comes from what is
inside them.

## 2. What is inside an atom

Every atom has two parts.

**The nucleus** is a tiny, dense lump in the center. It is made of two kinds of particle:

- **Protons**, which carry a positive electric charge.
- **Neutrons**, which carry no charge at all.

Protons and neutrons are almost exactly the same size and weight, and together they are called
**nucleons** ("things in the nucleus").

**The electrons** are much lighter particles — about 1/1,800 the weight of a proton — that move
around the nucleus in a cloud. Each electron carries a negative charge. An ordinary atom has the
same number of electrons as protons, so the charges cancel and the atom as a whole is neutral.

Here is the strange part. The nucleus holds nearly all of the atom's weight, but it takes up
almost none of its space. If an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be a
marble on the fifty-yard line, and the electrons would be a few gnats somewhere in the stands.
Everything else is empty. When you push on a table, what pushes back is the electric repulsion
between the electron clouds of your hand and the electron clouds of the table. You never get
anywhere near a nucleus.

Chemistry — burning, rusting, digesting, every reaction you have seen in a lab — happens in the
electron cloud. The nucleus just sits there. **Nuclear** science is about what happens when the
nucleus itself changes, and that is the subject of this site.

## 3. Two numbers describe every nucleus

**Z, the atomic number,** is the number of protons. This number decides which element the atom
is. One proton: hydrogen. Six: carbon. Twenty-six: iron. Ninety-two: uranium. Change the number of
protons and you have changed the element — there is no such thing as "carbon with seven protons";
that is nitrogen.

**A, the mass number,** is the total number of nucleons — protons plus neutrons. Since protons and
neutrons weigh about the same and electrons weigh almost nothing, A tells you roughly how heavy
the atom is.

The number of neutrons, which we call **N**, is what is left over:

> N = A − Z

For example, the most common kind of carbon atom has 6 protons and 6 neutrons, so Z = 6, N = 6,
and A = 12. Uranium-238 has Z = 92 and A = 238, so it has 238 − 92 = 146 neutrons.

The way scientists write a nucleus is the element's name or symbol followed by A: **carbon-12**,
**uranium-238**, or in symbols **C-12** and **U-238**. You will also see it written with the mass
number raised up in front of the symbol, like ²³⁵U. All of these mean the same thing. Notice that
you never have to write Z, because the element's name already tells you the number of protons.

## 4. The periodic table is a map

The **periodic table** is a chart of all the elements, arranged by Z — one proton for hydrogen in
the top left, then two for helium, and so on, up past ninety in the bottom rows.

It is not just a list. Its shape carries information:

- **Each row** (called a period) starts a new layer of the electron cloud. Elements in the same
  row have the same number of layers.
- **Each column** (called a group) holds elements whose outermost electrons are arranged the same
  way. Because chemistry is done by the outermost electrons, elements in the same column behave
  alike. The far-right column — helium, neon, argon — are gases that almost never react with
  anything. The far-left column — lithium, sodium, potassium — are soft metals that react
  violently with water.
- **The two long rows floated at the bottom** (the lanthanides and actinides) are really part of
  the sixth and seventh rows; they are drawn separately only so the table fits on a page. Uranium
  and plutonium live in the bottom one.

The table was built in the 1860s from chemistry alone, before anyone knew atoms had a nucleus.
Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the known elements by weight and by how they behaved, left gaps where
the pattern demanded an element nobody had found yet, and predicted what those missing elements
would be like. Three of them were discovered within his lifetime, with the properties he
predicted. It was later understood that the true ordering principle was Z, the proton count, and
that the repeating pattern comes from how electrons fill their layers.

## 5. What the periodic table does not show

Here is the limit of the map. The periodic table has **one box per element** — one box for
carbon, one for uranium. But an element's atoms do not all have the same number of neutrons.
Carbon with 6 neutrons and carbon with 8 neutrons are both carbon, both sit in the same box, and
both behave the same way in a chemistry lab. Yet one of them is stable forever and the other is
radioactive.

To see that difference you need a different map, with one box for every combination of protons
*and* neutrons. That map is called the **chart of the nuclides**, and it is the subject of the
next unit.

## Try it

- Pick three elements from the periodic table and, for each one, write down Z. Then look up the
  most common mass number A (a periodic table usually prints an average atomic mass; round it to
  the nearest whole number) and work out N = A − Z.
- Find uranium and plutonium on the table. Which row are they really part of?
- Look up **nucleon**, **atomic number**, and **mass number** in this site's [glossary](/glossary/).

## Where this comes from

Everything in this unit is standard introductory chemistry; any high-school chemistry textbook
covers it, usually in its first two or three chapters. The stadium comparison is a common
teaching picture: a nucleus is on the order of 10,000 to 100,000 times smaller across than the
atom it sits in. The story of Mendeleev's predictions is told in most histories of chemistry;
the three elements he predicted that were found in his lifetime are gallium (1875), scandium
(1879), and germanium (1886).
