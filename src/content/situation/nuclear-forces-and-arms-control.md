## World nuclear forces (SIPRI estimate for January 2026)

The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute's estimate for January 2026, published in
June 2026 [1]:

| State | Deployed | Stored | Military stockpile | Retired, awaiting dismantlement | Total inventory |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| United States | 1,770 | 1,930 | 3,700 | 1,342 | 5,042 |
| Russia | 1,796 | 2,604 | 4,400 | 1,020 | 5,420 |
| United Kingdom | 120 | 105 | 225 | – | 225 |
| France | 280 | 10 | 290 | 80 | 370 |
| China | 34 | 586 | 620 | – | 620 |
| India | 12 | 178 | 190 | . . | 190 |
| Pakistan | – | 170 | 170 | . . | 170 |
| North Korea | – | 60 | 60 | . . | 60 |
| Israel | – | 90 | 90 | . . | 90 |
| **World** | **4,012** | **5,733** | **9,745** | **2,442** | **12,187** |

Notation follows SIPRI: "–" means nil or negligible; ". ." means not available or not
applicable.

What changed from SIPRI's January 2025 estimate: the total inventory fell from 12,241 to 12,187
(retired U.S. and Russian warheads being dismantled), while the military stockpile — warheads
available for use — rose from 9,614 to 9,745 and deployed warheads from 3,912 to 4,012. SIPRI's
own summary: "the number of nuclear warheads in the world continues to decline, but this is only
due to Russia and the USA dismantling retired warheads," and "year-on-year increases can be seen
in the number of operational (stockpiled) nuclear warheads. This trend seems likely to continue
and will probably accelerate in the coming years" [1].

China's stockpile grew from about 600 to about 620 during 2025. On silos, SIPRI's chapter
reports the U.S. Department of Defense assessment of 2025 that China "had loaded more than 100
missiles into three new missile silo fields," and notes there is no public evidence that those
missiles are armed with nuclear warheads; SIPRI's June 2026 press release puts it as "hundreds of
missiles." This page uses the chapter's figure [1].

## Strategic arms control (status as of September 2026)

New START, the last treaty limiting U.S. and Russian strategic forces, expired on 5 February 2026
with no successor [2]. Its verification had already lapsed: on-site inspections stopped in March
2020 and were never resumed, and Russia's suspension of the treaty in February 2023 ended the
data exchanges [3]. Russia had proposed on 22 September 2025 that both sides keep observing the
treaty's central limits informally for one year; the United States did not formally respond, and
called instead for a new treaty covering more weapons and more parties. Russia's foreign minister
said in February 2026 that Russia would keep to the limits "only as long as the United States does
not exceed the above limits" [2]. SIPRI notes that the expiry "has eliminated that source of data
from the public debate" [1].

## Nuclear testing (status as of September 2026)

No explosive nuclear test has been confirmed by the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty
Organization's monitoring system since North Korea's in 2017. On 6 February 2026 the U.S. Under
Secretary of State for Arms Control, Thomas DiNanno, alleged that China "conducted one such
yield-producing nuclear test on June 22 of 2020"; China rejected the claim and said it had
"always honored its commitment to the moratorium on nuclear testing," and the CTBTO's executive
secretary said its monitoring system "did not detect any event consistent with the
characteristics of a nuclear weapon test explosion" at the time alleged [4]. On 30 October 2025
the U.S. President had stated that he had "instructed the Department of War to start testing our
Nuclear Weapons on an equal basis," without specifying whether explosive testing was meant [5];
SIPRI's 2026 yearbook records "concerns over the possible resumption of nuclear testing" [1]. The
CTBT remains signed but not in force.

## The treaty review cycles (status as of September 2026)

The Eleventh NPT Review Conference (New York, 28 April – 22 May 2026) ended without an agreed
final document — the third consecutive review conference to do so, after 2015 and 2022. The
dispute that blocked consensus was over a paragraph stating that Iran "can never seek, develop or
acquire any nuclear weapons," which the United States insisted on and Iran refused, and over the
absence of language condemning the June 2025 U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran's safeguarded
facilities. Non-nuclear-weapon states also objected that the draft asked the nuclear-armed states
only for "constructive dialogue" on disarmament [6].

The first Review Conference of the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons is scheduled for
30 November – 4 December 2026 in New York [7].

## Safeguards and the verification gap in Iran (status as of September 2026)

The IAEA's Safeguards Implementation Report for 2025, summarized in July 2026: 138 states had both
a comprehensive safeguards agreement and an Additional Protocol in force; for 75 of them the
Agency drew the broader conclusion that "all nuclear material remained in peaceful activities";
for 63 it could conclude only that "declared nuclear material remained in peaceful activities"
[8].

Iran, in sequence:

- **June 2025.** Israeli and U.S. strikes on Iran's safeguarded enrichment and related
  facilities. The Safeguards Implementation Report states that the Agency "was unable to verify"
  the nuclear material Iran had declared at the affected facilities and that "this nuclear
  material, which the Agency was not able to verify, includes a large amount of high enriched
  uranium" [8]. Iran had accumulated 440.9 kilograms of uranium enriched to up to 60 percent
  before the strikes [9].
- **28 February 2026.** A wider war between the United States and Israel on one side and Iran on
  the other began; it is described as ongoing [10]. The IAEA confirmed further strikes on the
  Natanz and Isfahan sites in early March 2026 [11].
- **June 2026.** A U.S.–Iran memorandum of understanding accompanying a ceasefire provides for
  IAEA inspector access and for down-blending of enriched uranium under IAEA supervision; the
  Director General said "the IAEA will have to have access and inspect." As of late June,
  inspections had not resumed, and Iran had indicated that the attacked sites would remain
  off-limits until a final agreement [12].
- **2 September 2026.** The Agency's report to its Board, as reported by RFE/RL, calls the lack
  of information about the material and of access to the facilities "a matter of proliferation
  concern" that "needs to be addressed with the utmost urgency" [9].
- **9 September 2026.** The Board of Governors adopted, by 23 votes to 3 with 8 abstentions
  (Russia, China, and Niger against), a resolution referring Iran to the UN Security Council over
  its non-compliance with its safeguards obligations. RFE/RL's report notes that the Agency "has
  lacked access to key Iranian nuclear facilities since Israeli and US strikes in June 2025" [13].
  What the Security Council did after the referral was not checked for this page.

## Attacks on civil nuclear facilities (status as of September 2026)

- **Zaporizhzhia, Ukraine.** Occupied by Russian forces since March 2022, with an IAEA presence
  on site; the plant's repeated loss of off-site power is the recurring safety concern. Details
  were not re-checked for this page beyond the fact of continued occupation [6].
- **Chernobyl, Ukraine.** On 14 February 2025 a drone with a warhead struck the New Safe
  Confinement over Unit 4 and opened a hole of more than 500 square feet in it [14]. On
  8 December 2025 the IAEA Director General said the structure "had lost its primary safety
  functions, including the confinement capability," while finding no permanent damage to its
  load-bearing structure [15].
- **Iran.** See the sequence above.

## Material out of regulatory control (ITDB, data through 2025, released March 2026)

The IAEA's Incident and Trafficking Database, with 145 participating states, held 4,626 incidents
for 1993–2025, of which 730 were thefts or attempted thefts; about 55 percent of those thefts
occurred during transport, rising to nearly 70 percent in the past decade. In 2025, 236 incidents
were reported by 34 states, up from 147 in 2024 — an increase the IAEA attributes to
retrospective reporting rather than to a real rise. Of the 4,626 incidents, 3,147 were in Group III, those
not connected, or unlikely to be connected, with trafficking [16].

## Regulatory status items referred from Module 1 (as of September 2026)

- **Fusion.** The NRC's proposed rule "Regulatory Framework for Fusion Machines" (*Federal
  Register*, 26 February 2026, Docket NRC-2023-0071) would regulate fusion machines under the 10 CFR Part 30
  byproduct-material framework. The comment period closed 27 May 2026. No final rule had been
  published as of this page's date [17].
- **Advanced-reactor demonstrations.** Under the Department of Energy's Reactor Pilot Program and
  Nuclear Energy Launch Pad, three DOE-authorized demonstration reactors reached first criticality
  in June–July 2026 (Antares Mark-0, Valar Ward 250, Deployable Energy Unity); these operate
  under DOE authorization, not NRC licenses [18].
