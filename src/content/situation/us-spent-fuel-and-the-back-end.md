*Compiled by the author for a public panel on 12 August 2026 and last re-checked on that date.
Every figure below carries its own as-of date and source. Items marked ⚠ are commonly misstated;
the correction is given. The site takes no position on whether the United States should recycle
its spent fuel.*

## Inventory (as of 31 December 2022 measured; June 2025 working figure)

- **About 95,000 metric tons** of commercial spent fuel (DOE working figure, June 2025 [1]); the
  last hard measurement was **90,963 MTHM in 314,878 assemblies** at the end of 2022 [2].
- Growing by **about 2,000 tons a year** [1][3]; DOE projects **85,000 more tons by 2070** [1].
- **Just over half is in dry casks** — 48,926 MTHM dry versus 42,037 in pools at the end of 2022;
  2021 was the first year dry storage exceeded pool storage [2].
- ⚠ Site counts depend on the denominator: 73 sites with reactors or storage installations, 20 of
  them with every reactor shut down (PNNL, end 2022); 75 sites in 33 states (GAO, 2021); 82
  NRC-licensed storage installations in 36 states (a licensing count, May 2025). The safe
  formulation is "roughly 75 sites in about a third of the states" [2][3].

## Yucca Mountain (status as of August 2026)

- The Nuclear Waste Policy Act of 1982, as amended in 1987, names Yucca Mountain as the only
  candidate repository and caps the first repository at **70,000 MTHM**, of which DOE allocated
  63,000 to commercial fuel — a figure the current inventory already exceeds by about half [4].
- DOE filed its license application in June 2008 and moved to withdraw it in 2010; the licensing
  board refused, the Commission split 2–2, and the proceeding was **suspended on 30 September 2011**
  for lack of funds. A federal court ordered the NRC to resume with remaining money in 2013.
- ⚠ **The safety review did not reject the site.** The NRC staff's Safety Evaluation Report
  (2014–15) found the application **met the post-closure standards over one million years**;
  what it found lacking was that DOE had not obtained the **land withdrawal and water rights**
  (10 CFR 63.121). Technically adequate, legally incomplete [5].
- The adjudication has never resumed. Finishing it would need on the order of **$330 million and
  hearings on up to 300 contentions** (NRC Chairman, 2015 testimony). No Yucca appropriation since
  FY2010; the surviving line is about $12 million a year for fund management and site upkeep.
- 2026 administration position: DOE's acting deputy assistant secretary says Yucca is "still the
  codified repository" but the Secretary "has made it clear it is not the solution"; no action has
  been taken to withdraw the application or repeal the designation. ⚠ "Yucca Mountain is dead" is a
  Nevada senator's phrase, not an administration statement [6].
- **4 August 2026:** Nevada's senators introduced S. 5233 to repeal the designation and require
  withdrawal of the application [7].

## The money (as of 30 September 2025)

**The breach.** Under the 1982 Act's Standard Contracts DOE was to begin taking fuel on
31 January 1998. It has accepted none. Damages are paid from the Treasury's Judgment Fund — by
taxpayers, not from the Nuclear Waste Fund — under a 2002 appellate ruling.

| Item | Amount | Source |
|---|---:|---|
| Paid to utilities to date (settlements + judgments) | **$12.2 billion** | DOE FY2025 Agency Financial Report [8] |
| Paid in FY2025 alone | $1.12 billion | same |
| Estimated **remaining** liability | **$38.6–44.3 billion** | same |
| ⚠ Total liability *including* amounts already paid | $50.8–56.5 billion | same — the upper figure is often misquoted as "remaining" |

DOE's estimate assumes Congress will both appropriate funds and amend the Act to permit
consolidated interim storage; if interim storage slips, the number grows.

**The Nuclear Waste Fund.** Ratepayers paid a fee of 0.1 cent per kilowatt-hour from 1983 until
a federal court ordered collection stopped in 2013 (DOE suspended it in May 2014). The Fund's
balance at 30 September 2025 was **$51.4 billion**, of which $21.8 billion was fees and most of the
rest Treasury interest; FY2025 income was $1.96 billion, almost entirely interest [9]. The Fund
cannot be spent without appropriation and sits on-budget, where its balance offsets the deficit.

## Private consolidated interim storage (status as of January 2026)

- Two facilities hold NRC licenses: **ISP/WCS in Andrews County, Texas** (40-year license,
  September 2021, up to 5,000 tons) and **Holtec HI-STORE in Lea County, New Mexico** (May 2023).
- The Fifth Circuit vacated both licenses in 2023–24; the Supreme Court reversed on procedural
  grounds in ***NRC v. Texas*** (18 June 2025, 6–3) without deciding whether the NRC has authority
  to license private away-from-reactor storage — that question is unresolved. Certiorari on the
  remaining Holtec challenge was denied 12 January 2026 [10].
- **Neither is being built.** Holtec cancelled HI-STORE in October 2025 after New Mexico's 2023
  statute barred storage without state consent; ISP has reportedly said it will not proceed
  without Texas's consent (trade press; not confirmed against a company source). The legal barrier
  is gone; **state consent is now the binding constraint.**

## The 2026 recycling push (status as of August 2026)

- **Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campuses.** DOE issued a request for information on 28 January
  2026; 28 applications came from 26 states; memoranda of understanding were signed with five
  states on 24 July 2026 (announced 28 July) — Tennessee among them. ⚠ The MOUs are non-binding;
  no bill in the 119th Congress authorizes the program; "DOE will pick three of five by the end of
  2026" is an Oklahoma state statement, not DOE's [11].
- **Statutory obstacles named by the program's own critics and supporters alike:** the Act caps
  DOE-provided interim storage at 1,900 tons (§135) and bars a monitored retrievable storage
  facility until a repository is licensed (§148); a governor's "consent" under §135 is a
  disapproval Congress may override, not a veto [4].
- **Regulatory gap for safeguards.** 10 CFR 74.51(a) excludes "an irradiated fuel reprocessing
  plant" by name from Category I material control and accounting requirements — a gap identified
  in 2009. The NRC's proposed one-step Part 70 licensing pathway for reprocessing (91 FR 38124,
  24 June 2026; comments closed 10 August 2026) and its companion physical-protection rule
  (91 FR 38928, 26 June 2026) address licensing and guards but **do not amend Part 74**. The NRC's
  own proposed rule says, in the present tense, "The gaps, however, remain" [12].
- **The DOE-authorization route.** Atomic Energy Act §110a exempts separation of special nuclear
  material done under contract for DOE from NRC licensing, and DOE's 22 April 2026 recycling
  solicitation explicitly offers DOE authorization "rather than NRC licensing." A facility on that
  route would sit outside 10 CFR Parts 73 and 74 and outside a public hearing [13].
- **Technology status.** PUREX is the only reprocessing process ever run at commercial scale;
  the largest U.S. demonstration of an advanced aqueous process on real commercial spent fuel
  totals about 4.5 kg; INL's pyroprocessing of EBR-II fuel has treated about 5.5 of 25 tons in 27
  years and "has not been developed past the engineering scale" (INL to NRC, December 2023) [14].
  Curio filed a letter of intent for a 4,000 t/yr plant on 27 May 2026; no license application has
  been submitted [15].

## Economics (prices as of 31 July 2026)

- Uranium closed July 2026 at **$86.38/lb U₃O₈, about $225/kg U** (Cameco spot) [16]. The
  standard break-even estimate for reprocessing versus direct disposal is above **$360/kg in 2003
  dollars, roughly $625–650 today** [17]; CRS (2025) finds closed cycles 5–20 percent more
  expensive and the once-through cycle cheaper for at least fifty years [18].
- Plant costs: DOE's estimate for Rokkasho-scale (800 t/yr) is about $20 billion; GAO scaled a
  3,000 t/yr U.S. plant to about $44 billion or more [19]. Rokkasho itself has had 27 postponements
  at an all-in cost of ¥18.66 trillion (about $117 billion), with full operation targeted for
  2032 [20]. THORP ran at about 60 percent and La Hague at about 65 percent of nameplate.
- The status quo costs about $1.1 billion a year in damages; a closed site's storage installation
  costs on the order of $10 million a year to keep (order of magnitude only) [8][21].

## International comparison (as of August 2026)

- **No country that reprocesses avoids a repository.** France (Cigéo, costed at €37 billion in
  2025 conditions; about 88 percent of its deep-disposal volume is long-lived intermediate waste,
  not vitrified HLW), the UK, Japan, and Russia all reprocess or did, and all are siting or
  building repositories [22].
- **Finland:** the regulator STUK issued its favorable safety statement on Onkalo on
  **4 August 2026**; the government's operating-license decision was expected in the autumn; no
  commercial fuel had been emplaced [23].
- **United Kingdom:** decided 24 January 2025 to immobilize about 117 of the 141 tons of separated
  plutonium at Sellafield rather than recycle it [24].
- **France** reprocesses only uranium-oxide fuel; spent MOX accumulates, and the regulator reports
  the plutonium stock growing [25].
