Every nuclear facility on the map sits somewhere on one of these two diagrams, and most of what
nuclear security does can be read off them.

**The civil cycle** takes uranium ore through milling, conversion to the gas uranium hexafluoride,
enrichment from the natural 0.7 percent of uranium-235 to the 3–5 percent a power reactor needs,
and fabrication into ceramic fuel pellets. After three to six years in a reactor the spent fuel is
intensely radioactive and is cooled in water, then stored in dry casks. In the *open* cycle that
is where it stays until geological disposal. In the *closed* cycle it is reprocessed: the
uranium and plutonium are chemically separated and returned to fuel fabrication, and the fission
products are immobilized in glass. Newer reactor designs shift the numbers — some use fuel
enriched to just under 20 percent (HALEU), some run on fuel forms other than pellets — but not
the shape of the cycle.

**The weapons routes** share the civil front end. The uranium route keeps enriching, from
reactor grade to 90 percent and above. The plutonium route runs natural or low-enriched fuel
through a reactor for a short time, so that the plutonium produced is mostly the isotope
plutonium-239, and then reprocesses it out. What makes a state's program worrying is therefore
not that it mines uranium or runs a reactor, but that it enriches beyond what any reactor needs,
or separates plutonium without a civil use for it. That is why the international safeguards
system spends its effort on those two steps, and why the map marks enrichment and reprocessing
plants distinctly.

**A caution on reading the map.** The presence of a facility says nothing by itself about
intent: France reprocesses on an industrial scale for its civil program, several countries enrich
for the commercial fuel market, and research reactors run on every continent. The diagrams show
*capability*; the safeguards record — declarations, inspections, and the IAEA's conclusions —
is what speaks to *use*.
